# Subsequences (Sec-3.4)

> [!def] Definition: Subsequences
> Let $X=(x_n)$ be a sequence of real numbers and let $n_1 < n_2 < ...$ be a strictly increasing sequence of natural numbers.
> Then the sequence $(x_{n_k})_{k=1}$ given by $x_{n_1}, x_{n_2}, ...$ is a subsequence.
> Then the following relationships hold:
> $$	\begin{align}
> 		 & X: \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}        \\
> 		 & X|_A: A \to \mathbb{R} \text{ where } A \subset \mathbb{N}
> 	\end{align}$$
> 
> 

> [!case] Example
> If $X = \frac{1}{n}$, then $\frac{1}{2n}$ can be viewed as a subsequence of $X$.
> 
> $$1/2, ~1/4, ~1/6, ~1/8, ...$$
> 
> 
> $$(\frac{1}{2n}) \text{ can also be viewed as } \frac{1}{2}X$$
> 
> 

> [!thm] Theorem: Subsequence Convergence
> If $X$ converges to a real number $L$, then any subsequence of $X$ also converges to $L$.
> ^subsequence-convergence

> [!hum] Aside: Understanding Subsequence Convergence
> Let $x_{n_k}$ be a subsequence of $X$.
> $\implies |x_n - L | < \epsilon, \forall ~n \geq n_\epsilon$.
> Our goal now is to satisfy: $|x_{n_k} - L| < \epsilon \forall ~k \geq k_{\epsilon}$.
> To do this we must find a $k_{\epsilon}$ for which the inequality holds.
> This is also saying $\exists ~k_{\epsilon} > 0, n_k \geq n_{\epsilon} \forall ~k \geq k_{\epsilon}$.
> $$	\begin{gather}
> 		\text{Since } x_n \rightarrow L, \forall ~\epsilon > 0, \exists ~n_{\epsilon} > 0 \ni |x_n - L| < \epsilon, \forall ~n \geq n_{\epsilon} \\
> 		n_k  \text{ is a monotonic sequence of } \mathbb{N} \implies \exists ~k_\epsilon > 0 \ni n_k \geq n_{\epsilon} \forall ~ k \geq k_{\epsilon} \\
> 		\text{Then } \forall ~ k \geq k_{\epsilon}, |x_{n_k} - x | < \epsilon
> 	\end{gather}$$
> 
> 

> [!imp] Important: The Contrapositive
> Consider: If any subsequence of $X$ converges to $L$, then $x_n \rightarrow L$.
> Contrapositive: If $x_n \not \rightarrow L$ then there exists a subsequence of $X$ that does NOT converge to $L$.

# Divergence of a Sequence

> [!thm] Theorem: Divergence Equivalence
> Let $X$ be a sequence, then the following are equivalent:
> 1. $X$ does not converge to $L \in \mathbb{R}$.
> 2. $\exists ~ \epsilon_0 > 0 \ni \forall ~k \in \mathbb{N}, \exists ~n_k \in \mathbb{N} \ni n_k \geq k \land |x_{n_k} - L| \geq \epsilon_0$ (Negation of 1).
> 3. $\exists ~\epsilon_0$ and a subsequence $x_{n_k}$ of $X$ s.t. $|x_{n_k}-L| \geq \epsilon_0, \forall ~k \in \mathbb{N}$.
> 
> 

> [!pf] Proof
> **($3 \implies 1$):**
> Is solved via the relationship of sequence-subsequence convergence.
> **($2 \implies 3$):**
> * Let $\epsilon_0$ be in $(ii)$ for 1, $n_1 \in \mathbb{N} \ni n_1 \geq 1 \land |x_{n_1} - L| \geq \epsilon$.
> * For $n_1+1 \in \mathbb{N}, \exists ~n_2 \in \mathbb{N}$ s.t. $n_0 \geq n_1+1$ and $|x_{n_2}-L| \geq \epsilon$.
> * For $n_2+1 \in \mathbb{N}, \exists ~n_3 \in \mathbb{N}$ s.t. $n_0 \geq n_2+1$ and $|x_{n_3}-L| \geq \epsilon$.
> * ... continues.
> * For $n_k+1 \in \mathbb{N}, \exists ~n_{k+1} \in \mathbb{N}$ s.t. $n_0 \geq n_k+1$ and $|x_{n_{k+1}}-L| \geq \epsilon$.
> 
> 
> So $n_k$ is strictly increasing then $x_{n_k}$ is a subsequence of $X$ and $|x_{n_k}-L| \geq \epsilon_0, \forall ~ n \in \mathbb{N}$.

# Unbounded Sequences

> [!thm] Theorem: Unbounded Sequences and Zero-Limit Subsequences
> Show that: If $X$ is unbounded, then $\exists$ a subsequence $x_{n_k}$ s.t. $\lim_{k \to \infty} \frac{1}{x_{n_k}} = 0$.

> [!def] Recall: Bounded vs. Unbounded
> * **Bounded:** $\exists ~M>0 \ni |x_n| \leq M ~\forall ~n \in \mathbb{N} \implies X \text{ is bounded}$.
> * **Unbounded:** $\forall ~k \in \mathbb{N}, \exists ~n_k \in \mathbb{N} \ni |x_{n_k}| > k \implies X \text{ is unbounded}$.
> * *Note:* if $\forall ~m \geq K, |x_m| \leq k$ then the sequence would be bounded. $\implies n_k \geq k$.
> 
> 
> 
> 

> [!pf] Proof
> $$\begin{gathered}
> \frac{1}{x_{n_k}} \rightarrow 0 \\
> |\frac{1}{x_{n_k}} - 0| < \epsilon \\
> |\frac{1}{x_{n_k}}| < \epsilon \\
> \forall ~\epsilon_0, \exists ~k(\epsilon) \ni \left|\frac{1}{x_{n_k}}\right| > \frac{1}{\epsilon} , \forall ~k \geq k(\epsilon)
> \end{gathered}$$
> 
> 
> $$\forall ~\epsilon > 0, k=1, \exists ~n_1 \in \mathbb{N} \ni |x_{n_1}| > 1$$
> 
> 
> For $n_1+1 ~\exists ~n_2 \ni |x_{n_2}|>n_2 \land n_2 \geq  n_1+1>n_1$.
> 
> $$\vdots$$
> 
> 
> $$n_k+1 ~\exists ~n_{k+1} \ni |x_{n_{k+1}}| > n_{k+1} \land n_{k+1} \geq  n_k+1>n_k$$
> 
> 
> So we can find a subsequence $(x_{n_k})$ s.t. $|x_{n_k}| > n_{k-1} \forall k \geq 2$.
> $$\forall  \epsilon > 0, \exists  M > \frac{1}{\epsilon} \text{ since } n_k \text{ is strictly increasing } \exists k(\epsilon) >0 \ni n_{k-1} > M \forall k \geq k(\epsilon) \implies |x_{n_k}| > \frac{1}{\epsilon}$$
> 
>

# Divergence Criteria

> [!?] Question: Finding a Sequence
> Find a subsequence of $X$ s.t. $|x_{n+k}| > k \forall  ~k \in  \mathbb{N}$.
> 
> $$0 < \frac{1}{x_{n+k}} < \frac{1}{k}$$
> 
> 

> [!thm] Theorem: Divergence Criteria
> $X$ is divergent if:
> 1. $X$ has two convergent subsequences $X'= (x_{n_k})$ and $X''= (x_{m_k})$ whose limits are not equal.
> 2. $X$ is unbounded.
> 
>

## Examples

> [!lem] Example: Limit of $b^n$
> 
> $$0<b<1 \implies \lim_{n \to \infty} b^n=0$$
> 
> 

> [!pf] Proof
> Since $(x_n = b^n)$ is decreasing and bounded below by zero, by the monotone convergence theorem $(x_n)$ has a limit, denoted by $x$.
> Note that: $(b^{2n})$ is a subsequence of $(b^{n})$ then $b^{2n} \rightarrow k$.
> Note that: $(b^n)^2 = b^{2n} \implies b^{2n} \rightarrow x$.
> So $x=x^2 \land b^n < b^1 = b<1 \implies x=0$ that is $\lim_{n \to \infty} b^n = 0$.
