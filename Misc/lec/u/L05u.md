> [!pf]- 
> Let $S = \{ s \in \mathbb{R} | 0 \leq S | s^2 < 2\}$
> 
> If $S$ is:
> - **Property 1:** $S \neq \emptyset$
> - **Property 2:** Bounded above $\implies \exists ~\sup S$ by the [Completeness Property of R](1767467862-FFER.md)
> 
> 
> 
> Note: as $0 \in S$ since $0^2 < 2$ thus $S \neq \emptyset$.
> 
> Now we must find an upper bound of $S$
> 
> > [!pf] Consider $2$: We must show $\forall ~s \in S, s < 2$.
> > Suppose $\exists ~s \in S$ s.t. $s > 2$. 
> > - If $s > 2 \implies s^2 > 2s \implies  s^2 > 2s > 2 \cdot 2 = 4$
> > 
> > This is a contradiction.
> 
> Thus, $2$ is an upper bound of $S \implies \exists ~x \ni x = \sup S$  by the [Completeness Property](L04.md)
> 
> By the [Trichotony Property](1766962952-MAQA.md) we know either:
> $$
>     x^2 < 2 \quad x^2 = 2 \quad x^2 > 2
> $$ 
> We want to show this impossibility $x^2 < 2$:
> $$
>     x \in S, (x+\epsilon)^2 < 2
> $$ 
> This is impossible as if $(x+\epsilon)^2 < 2$ then there would be another element in $S$ that is larger than $\sup S$
> 
> [!pf]
> 
> For the sake of contradiction suppose;
> $$
>     x^2 < 2, \exists ~n \in \mathbb{N} \text{ s.t. } x+\frac{1}{n} \in S \quad\quad\text{ By showing that } (x+\frac{1}{n})^2 < 2
> $$ 
> 
> Observe the following:
> $$
> \begin{align}
>     (x+\frac{1}{n})^2 = x^2 + \frac{2x}{n} + \frac{1}{n^2} \\
> \end{align}
> $$
> Thus:
> $$
> \begin{align}
>       \frac{1}{n^2} < \frac{1}{n} \\
> n^2 > n \\
> n \geq 1
> \end{align}
> $$ 
> 
> Consider backwards search:
> $$
> \begin{align}
> (x+\frac{1}{n})^2 = x^2 + \frac{2x}{n} + \frac{1}{n^2} \\
> x^2 + \frac{2x}{n} + \frac{1}{n^2} \leq x^2 + \frac{2x}{n} + \frac{1}{n} \\
> = x^2 + \frac{2x+1}{n} < 2 \\
> \iff \frac{2x+1}{n} < 2-x  \\
> \iff \frac{1}{2x^2}\frac{2x+1}{n} < 2-x  \frac{1}{2x^2}\\
> \iff \frac{2x+1}{2-x^2} < n
> \end{align}
> $$ 
> 
> So by [Archimedean Property](1767464355-HTFW.md) $\exists ~n \in \mathbb{N}$ s.t. $n > \frac{2x+1}{2-x^2}$
> 
> Thus $\frac{1}{2x^2}\frac{2x+1}{n} < 2-x  \frac{1}{2x^2}$
> 
> Thus $\frac{2x+1}{n} < 2-x$
> 
> Therefore we can say $x^2 + \frac{2x+1}{n} < 2$  which can't be can't be the supremum of $S$
> 
> ---
> For $x^2>2$: We want to disprove $(x-\epsilon)^2 > 2$.
> We must show $x-\epsilon$ is an upperbound of $S$. (Note: $\epsilon = \frac{1}{n}$)
> For the sake of contradiction suppose $x-\frac{1}{n} \geq  s$
> 
> Consider backwards search:
> $$
> \begin{align}
>     \forall ~s \in S, x-\frac{1}{n} \geq s \\
> \exists ~s \in S \text{ s.t. } \\
> x-\frac{1}{n} < s \\
> \implies  2 < (x-\frac{1}{n})^2 < s^2 < 2
> \end{align}
> $$ 
> 
> $$
> \begin{align}
> (x-\frac{1}{n})^2 = x^2 - \frac{2x}{n} + \frac{1}{n} \\ 
> x^2 - \frac{2x}{n} + \frac{1}{n} > x^2 - \frac{2x}{n} > 2 \\
> x^2-2 > \frac{2x}{n} \\
> n > \frac{2x}{x^2 - 2}
> \end{align}
> $$ 
> 
> Forward Search:
> By the [Archimedean Property](1767464355-HTFW.md) $\exists ~n \in \mathbb{N} \ni$:
> $$
> \begin{align}
>     \frac{2x}{x^2-2} < n_x \implies \frac{2x}{n} < x^2 - 2 - \frac{2x}{n} \implies 2 < x^2 - \frac{2x}{n}\\
> \text{Note: } (x-\frac{1}{n})^2 = x^2 - \frac{2x}{n} + \frac{1}{n^2} > 2 \\
> \text{Show that } x-\frac{1}{n} \text{ is an upper bound of } S \\
> \text{Suppose that } \exists ~ s \in S \text{ s.t. } x-\frac{1}{n} < s \\
> \implies (x-\frac{1}{n})^2 < s^2 (\text{ We can assume that } \frac{1}{n} < x)
> \end{align}
> $$ 
> 
> Then $2 < (x - \frac{1}{n})^2 < s^2 < 2$ which is a contradiction! 
> 
> Therefore $x-\frac{1}{n}$ is an upper bound of $S$, this is a contradiction to the fact that $x = \sup S$
