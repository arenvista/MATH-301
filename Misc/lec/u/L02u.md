# Section 1.3 — Countable and Infinite Sets

## Definitions

> [!def] **Finite (Countable) Set with $n$ Elements**
> A set $S$ is said to have **$n$ elements** if there exists a bijection
> $$
> f : {1,2,\dots,n} \to S.
> $$

---

> [!def] **Denumerable Set**
> A set $S$ is **denumerable** if it is infinite and there exists a bijection
> $$
> f : \mathbb{N} \to S.
> $$

---

## Properties of Countable Sets

> [!thm] **Intersection and Union of Countable Sets**
> Let $S$ and $T$ be sets.
>
> 1. If one of $S$ or $T$ is countable, then $S \cap T$ is countable.
> 2. If both $S$ and $T$ are countable, then $S \cup T$ is countable.

---

### Constructing a Bijection for a Union

> [!thm] **Union of Two Denumerable Sets is Denumerable**
> If $S$ and $T$ are denumerable, then $S \cup T$ is denumerable.

> [!pf]
> Let
> $$
> f:\mathbb{N} \to S, \qquad g:\mathbb{N} \to T
> $$
> be bijections.
>
> Since $S$ and $T$ may overlap, write
> $$
> S \cup T = S \cup (T \setminus S).
> $$
>
> Define
> $$
> h:\mathbb{N} \to S \cup T
> $$
> by interlacing:
> $$
> h(n)=
> \begin{cases}
> f(k), & n=2k \text{ (even)} \
> g(k), & n=2k-1 \text{ (odd)}.
> \end{cases}
> $$
>
> This enumerates elements alternately from $S$ and $T \setminus S$, producing a bijection. ∎

---

## Equivalent Characterizations of Countability

> [!thm] **Equivalent Definitions of Countable Sets**
> The following are equivalent:
>
> 1. $S$ is countable.
> 2. There exists a surjection $\mathbb{N} \twoheadrightarrow S$.
> 3. There exists an injection $S \hookrightarrow \mathbb{N}$.

---

## The Rational Numbers are Countable

> [!thm] **$\mathbb{Q}$ is Countable**

> [!pf]
> Write
> $$
> \mathbb{Q} = \mathbb{Q}^+ \cup {0} \cup \mathbb{Q}^-.
> $$
>
> Consider
> $$
> \mathbb{Q}^+ = \left{ \frac{m}{n} \mid m,n \in \mathbb{N} \right}.
> $$
>
> Arrange these in an infinite grid:
>
> |     |     |     |     |   |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- | - |
> | 1/1 | 2/1 | 3/1 | 4/1 | … |
> | 1/2 | 2/2 | 3/2 | 4/2 | … |
> | 1/3 | 2/3 | 3/3 | 4/3 | … |
> | …   | …   | …   | …   | … |
>
> Traversing along diagonals enumerates all positive rationals (removing duplicates such as $2/2$).
> Hence $\mathbb{Q}^+$ is countable.
>
> Similarly $\mathbb{Q}^-$ is countable, and adding ${0}$ preserves countability.
> Therefore $\mathbb{Q}$ is countable. ∎

---

> [!thm] **Countable Union of Countable Sets**
> If each $A_n$ is countable, then
> $$
> \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} A_n
> $$
> is countable.

> [!pf]
> For each $n$, let
> $$
> f^n:\mathbb{N} \to A_n
> $$
> be a bijection.
>
> Arrange the values in an infinite array:
>
> * Row 1: $f^1(1), f^1(2), f^1(3), \dots$
> * Row 2: $f^2(1), f^2(2), f^2(3), \dots$
> * Row $n$: $f^n(1), f^n(2), f^n(3), \dots$
>
> Enumerating diagonally lists all elements in the union, proving countability. ∎

# Section 2.1 — The Real Numbers

## Algebraic Structure of $\mathbb{R}$

> [!def] **Field Properties of $\mathbb{R}$**
> The set $\mathbb{R}$ is equipped with two binary operations:
>
> * Addition $(+)$
> * Multiplication $(\cdot)$

---

### Addition Axioms

1. Commutativity: $a+b=b+a$
2. Associativity: $(a+b)+c=a+(b+c)$
3. Identity: $\exists,0$ such that $a+0=a$
4. Inverses: $\forall a,\exists (-a)$ with $a+(-a)=0$

---

### Multiplication Axioms

1. Commutativity: $ab=ba$
2. Associativity: $(ab)c=a(bc)$
3. Identity: $\exists,1$ such that $a\cdot1=a$
4. Inverses: $\forall a\neq0,\exists \frac1a$ with $a\cdot\frac1a=1$

---

### Distributive Law

$$
a(b+c)=ab+ac.
$$

---

## Fundamental Algebraic Results

> [!thm] **Uniqueness of the Additive Identity**
> If $z+a=a$, then $z=0$.
>
> > [!pf]
> > $$
> > \begin{aligned}
> > z+a &= a \
> > (z+a)+(-a) &= a+(-a) \
> > z+(a+(-a)) &= 0 \
> > z+0 &= 0 \
> > z &= 0.
> > \end{aligned}
> > $$

---

> [!cor] **Cancellation Law (Multiplication)**
> If $a\cdot b=a\cdot c$ and $a\neq0$, then $b=c$.

---

> [!thm] **Zero Multiplication Property**
> $a\cdot0=0$ for all $a\in\mathbb{R}$.

---

> [!thm] **Zero Product Property**
> If $ab=0$, then $a=0$ or $b=0$.

> [!pf]
> If $a\neq0$, multiply both sides by $\frac1a$:
> $$
> \frac1a(ab)=\frac1a\cdot0
> $$
> giving $b=0$. ∎

# Rational Numbers

> [!def] **Rational Numbers**
> $$
> \mathbb{Q}=\left{\frac{m}{n}\mid m,n\in\mathbb{Z},;n\neq0\right}.
> $$

---

## Existence of Irrational Numbers

> [!thm] **$\sqrt{2}$ is Irrational**
> There does not exist $r\in\mathbb{Q}$ such that $r^2=2$.

> [!pf] *Proof by contradiction.*
>
> Suppose $r=\frac{m}{n}$ in lowest terms and
> $$
> \left(\frac{m}{n}\right)^2=2.
> $$
> Then
> $$
> m^2=2n^2.
> $$
> Hence $m$ is even, say $m=2k$.
>
> Substituting:
> $$
> n^2=2k^2,
> $$
> so $n$ is even.
>
> Thus $m,n$ share a factor of 2, contradicting lowest terms.
> Therefore $\sqrt2$ is irrational. ∎

# Ordering Properties of $\mathbb{R}$

## Positive Sets

> [!def] **Set of Positive Real Numbers**
> A nonempty subset $P\subset\mathbb{R}$ is called the set of positive numbers if:
>
> 1. If $a,b\in P$, then $a+b\in P$.
> 2. If $a,b\in P$, then $ab\in P$.
> 3. (**Trichotomy**) For every $a\in\mathbb{R}$ exactly one holds:
>
>    * $a\in P$
>    * $a=0$
>    * $-a\in P$

---

## Order Relations

> [!def] **Order on $\mathbb{R}$**
>
> * $a>b$ if $a-b\in P$
> * $a\ge b$ if $a-b\in P\cup{0}$

For any $a,b\in\mathbb{R}$ exactly one holds:

* $a>b$
* $a<b$
* $a=b$
