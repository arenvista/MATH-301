# Intervals

# Nested Intervals
> [!def] Nested Intervals
> We say a sequence of intervals is _nested_ if:
> $$ \subseteq I*{n+1} \subseteq I_n \subseteq ~... \subseteq I_3 \subseteq I_2 \subseteq I_1 $$
> $$ \cap^{\infty}*{n=1} I_n = ~? $$

# Properties of $I_n$
> [!thm] Properties of $I_n$
>
> $$
> 	\begin{align}
> & I_n = (0,\frac{1}{n}) \implies \{ I_n \} \text{ is nested} \implies \cap^{\infty}_{n=1} I_n = \emptyset \\
> & I_n = [0,\frac{1}{n}] \implies \{ I_n \} \text{ is nested} \implies \cap^{\infty}_{n=1} I_n = \{ 0 \}
> \end{align}
> $$
>
> $$ \implies ~I_n \text{ must be closed.} $$


# Boundedness of Intervals
> [!cor] Boundedness of Intervals
> $$ \text{Consider: } I*n = [n, \infty) \text{ is closed} $$
> $$ \text{Can the following exists?: } a \in \cap^{\infty}*{n=1} I_n \\ $$
> Consider [Archimedean Property](1767464355-HTFW.md):
> $$ \exists ~n \in \mathbb{N} \ni a < n \implies a \not \in [n,\infty) $$
> $$ \therefore I_n \text{ must be bounded.} $$


# Nested Intervals Property
> [!thm] Nested Intervals Property
> If $I_n = [a_n, b_n] n \in \mathbb{N}$ is a nested sequence of closed and bounded interval.
> $\implies$ Then there exists a number $\xi \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $\xi \in \cap^{\infty}_{n=1} I_n$
>
> - $\implies$ That $\xi$ must be an _upper bound_ of the left endpoints ($A = \{ a_n, n \in \mathbb{N} \}$)
> - Also $\xi$ is a _lower bound_ of the left endpoints ($B = \{ b_n, n \in \mathbb{N} \}$).
> - We can consider the $\sup A$ as a candidate.
> - If $\sup A$ is not a lower bound of $B$ there there will not exist a $\xi$ that can be a member of $\cap^{\infty}_{n=1} I_n$
>
> ## Nested Intervals (Proof)
> > [!pf] Proof
> > Since $\forall ~n \in \mathbb{N}, a_n \leq b_n \leq b_1 \implies A$ is nonempty and bounded above.
> > $\implies$ by the [Completeness Property of R](1767467862-FFER.md) $A$ holds
> > $\implies \exists ~\xi := \sup A \in \mathbb{R}$
> >
> > _Goal:_ Show $\forall ~n \in \mathbb{N}, \xi \leq b_n$
> >
> > Let $m \in \mathbb{N}$. Suppose $\exists ~b_m < \xi$. _To get the contradiction we show $\forall ~n \in \mathbb{N}, m \geq  a_n$_
> >
> > $$
> > \text{Observe the following: }
> > \begin{cases}
> > 		\forall ~i \leq m & b_m \geq  a_m \geq  a_i \\
> > 		\forall ~i>m      & a_i \leq  b_i \leq  b_m
> > \end{cases}
> > $$
> >
> > Thus for $i>m$ $a_i \leq b_I \leq b_m$ so $b_m \geq  a_i$ for all $i \in \mathbb{N}$. Thus $b_m$ is an upper bound of $a_n, n \in \mathbb{N}$. This contradicts the fact that $\xi$ is the least upper bound of $A$. Thus we must take the original proposition: $\forall ~n \in \mathbb{N}, \xi \leq b_n$.
> >
> > $$ \therefore \xi \in \cap^{\infty}\_{n=1} I_n = [\sup A, \inf B] $$

> [!cor] Uniqueness of $\xi$
> $$ inf \{ b*n-a_n | n \in \mathbb{N} \} = 0 \implies \exists ~\xi! \in \cap^{\infty}*{n=1} I_n $$

# Sequences
> [!def] Sequences
> A sequence of real numbers (a sequence in $\mathbb{R}$) is a mapping from $\mathbb{N}$ onto a subset of $\mathbb{R}$ denoted by:
>
> $$
> 	X: \mathbb{N} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}
> $$
>
> The value of $X$ at $n \in \mathbb{N}$ is denoted by $X(n)$ or $x_n$. The sequence is denoted by:
>
> $$
> 	\begin{align}
>                    & X, \quad (x_n), \quad \{ x_n, n \in \mathbb{N} \}   \\
> 		\text{Example: } & ((-1)^n), \quad (\frac{1}{n}), \quad (\frac{1}{2n})
> \end{align}
> $$
>
> There are some sequences that can only be defined via induction/recursion.
>
> $$
>     \text{Example: (Fibonacci Sequence) } x_1-1, x_2=1, x_{n+2} = x_n + x_{n+1}
> $$

# Limit of a Sequence

## Convergence
> [!def] Convergence
> A sequence $(x_n)$ is said to converge to $x \in \mathbb{R}$
>
> $x$ is a limit of $\{ x_n, n \in \mathbb{N} \}, \forall ~\epsilon>0 ~\exists ~N(\epsilon) \in \mathbb{N}$ s.t. $\forall ~n \in N(\epsilon), |x_n-x| < \epsilon$ holds.
>
> $$ (|x_n-x| < \epsilon) \quad \text{ is the $\epsilon$-neighborhoor of $x$} $$
>
> $$
> \text{Observe $|x_n-x|$} :=
> \left \{
> \begin{array}{c}
> \equiv & x-\epsilon < x_n < x+\epsilon \\
> \equiv & -\epsilon < x_n-x < \epsilon  \\
> \equiv & |x_n - x | < \epsilon
> \end{array}
> \right .
> $$
>
> If a sequence has a limit then the sequence _converges_; else it is _divergent_.
>
> $$
> \begin{align}
> \text{If $\{ x_n \}$ converges to $x$ we denote it as: } \\
> \lim_{n \to \infty} x_n = x_1 \text{ or } x_n \rightarrow x \text{ as } n \rightarrow \infty
> \end{align}
> $$
>
> ## Visualizing Convergence

# Limits are Unique
> [!cor] Limits are Unique
> Consider the case when there are two limits:
>
> $$
> \begin{align}
> n \geq N(\epsilon) \exists ~x_n \\
> n > N_j(\epsilon)               \\exists ~x_n \\
> \implies \exists ~
> \end{align}
> $$

# Example: Convergenece of $\frac{1}{n}$
> [!pf] Example: Convergenece of $\frac{1}{n}$
> Consider the example:
>
> $$
> \text{Prove: } \quad \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} = 0
> $$
>
> By backwards search:
>
> $$
> 	\begin{align}
> 		 & \forall ~\epsilon > 0, \exists ~N(\epsilon) \in \mathbb{N}                                       \\
> 		 & \forall ~ n \in \mathbb{N}(\epsilon), |\frac{1}{n} - 0 | < \epsilon                              \\
> 		 & \equiv \frac{1}{n}<\epsilon                                                                      \\
> 		 & \equiv n > \frac{1}{\epsilon} \text{ if } n \geq  N(\epsilon)                                    \\
> 		 & \leftarrow N(\epsilon) > \frac{1}{\epsilon} \\
> 		 & \leftarrow \text{Archimedean Property}
> 	\end{align}
> $$
>
> Forward Logic:
>
> $$
> \begin{align}
> & \text{Observe } \frac{1}{\epsilon} \in \mathbb{R} \\
> & \implies \text{Archimedean Property Holds} \\
> & \implies  \exists ~ N(\epsilon) \in \mathbb{N} \text{ s.t. } N(\epsilon) > \frac{1}{\epsilon} \\
> & \implies \forall ~n \in N(\epsilon), n > \frac{1}{\epsilon} \\
> & \implies \frac{1}{n} < \epsilon \implies |\frac{1}{n}-0| < \epsilon \\
> & \therefore \frac{1}{n} \rightarrow 0 \text{ as } n \rightarrow \infty
> \end{align}
> $$

# Example: Convergence of $(\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt{n})$
> [!pf] Example: Convergence of $(\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt{n})$
>
> $$
>     \lim_{n \to \infty} (\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt{n}) = \infty
> $$
>
> ## Recall: (Conjugates)
> > [!tip] Recall: (Conjugates)
> >
> > $$
> > \begin{align}
> > & \sqrt{a} - \sqrt{b} =\frac{(\sqrt{a} - \sqrt{b}) (\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b})}{\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b}} = \frac{a-b}{\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}}
> > \end{align}
> > $$
>
> Applying Conjugates:
>
> $$
> \begin{align}
> (\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt{n}) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}+\sqrt{n}}
> \end{align}
> $$
>
> Consider backwards search:
>
> $$
> \begin{align}
> & \forall ~ \epsilon > 0, \exists ~N(\epsilon) \ni \forall ~ n \geq  N(\epsilon) \\
> & \iff  |\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}+ \sqrt{n}}-0| < \epsilon \\
> & \iff \frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}+ \sqrt{n}} < \epsilon \\
> & \leftarrow \frac{1}{2 \sqrt{n}} < \epsilon \\
> & (\text{So we are asking does: } \frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}+\sqrt{n}} < \frac{1}{2 \sqrt{n}} \text{ hold?}) \\
> & \leftarrow \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} < 2 \epsilon  \\
> & \iff \frac{1}{2 \epsilon} < \sqrt{n}  \\
> & \iff n > (\frac{1}{2 \epsilon})^2 \\
> & \leftarrow \text{ Holds By Archimedean Property}
> \end{align}
> $$
>
> Forward Logic:
>
> $$
> \begin{align}
> & \text{ By Archimedean Property } \\
> & \implies \exists ~N(\epsilon) \in \mathbb{N} \text{ s.t. } N(\epsilon) > (\frac{1}{2 \epsilon})^2 \\
> & \implies \forall ~n \geq N(\epsilon), n > (\frac{1}{2 \epsilon})^2 \\
> & \implies \sqrt{n} > \frac{1}{2 \epsilon} \implies \frac{1}{2 \sqrt{n}} < \epsilon \implies  \frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}+\sqrt{n}} < \epsilon \\
> & \implies | \frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1} + \sqrt{n}} - 0 < \epsilon
> \end{align}
> $$

# Divergence
> [!def] Divergence
>
> $$
> \text{
> $x_n$ does not converge $x$ if $\exists ~\epsilon_0 > 0$ s.t. $\forall ~ n \in \mathbb{N}$, $\exists ~ m_x \geq  n$ and $|x_{m_n} - x| \geq  \epsilon$
> }
> $$

# Tail of Sequences

> [!def] Tail of Sequences
> $$ \text{If $X = (x_1, x_2, ...)$ is a sequence of real numbers and $m \in \mathbb{N}$ then the \textbf{m-tail} of $X$ is the sequence: } $$
> $$ X*m := \{ x*{m+n}, n \in \mathbb{N} \} = \{ x*{m+1}, x*{m+2}, ..., x\_{m+n} \} $$
> $$ \text{ $X$ converges to $x$ $\iff$ $X_m$ converges to $x$ } $$
