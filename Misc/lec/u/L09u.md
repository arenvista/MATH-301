# Subsequences (Sec. 3.4)

## Definition and Notation

> [!def] Definition: Subsequences
> Let $X = (x_n)$ be a sequence of real numbers and let $n_1 < n_2 < \cdots$ be a strictly increasing sequence in $\mathbb{N}$.  
> The sequence $(x_{n_k})_{k=1}^\infty = x_{n_1}, x_{n_2}, \ldots$ is called a subsequence of $X$.
>
> Function view:
> $$
> X:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad X|_A: A\to\mathbb{R}\text{ for }A\subset\mathbb{N}
> $$

> [!case] Example
> If $x_n = \tfrac{1}{n}$, then $x_{2n} = \tfrac{1}{2n}$ is a subsequence (choose $n_k = 2k$).
>
> The same sequence can be written as a pointwise scaling: $(\tfrac{1}{2})X = \big(\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot \tfrac{1}{n}\big)_{n=1}^\infty = \big(\tfrac{1}{2n}\big)_{n=1}^\infty$.

## Subsequence Convergence

> [!thm] Theorem: Subsequence Convergence
> If $x_n \to L\in\mathbb{R}$, then every subsequence $x_{n_k}$ also converges to $L$.

> [!pf] Proof (Sketch)
> Fix $\epsilon>0$. Since $x_n\to L$, there exists $n_\epsilon$ such that $|x_n - L|<\epsilon$ for all $n\ge n_\epsilon$.  
> Because $(n_k)$ is strictly increasing, there exists $k_\epsilon$ with $n_k \ge n_\epsilon$ for all $k\ge k_\epsilon$.  
> Hence, for $k\ge k_\epsilon$, we have $|x_{n_k}-L|<\epsilon$, so $x_{n_k}\to L$.

> [!imp] Important: Contrapositive and Equivalences
> - Contrapositive (useful test): If there exists a subsequence of $X$ that does not converge to $L$, then $x_n$ does not converge to $L$.
> - Equivalently: $x_n\to L$ if and only if every subsequence of $X$ converges to $L$.


# Divergence of a Sequence

## Equivalent Characterizations

> [!thm] Theorem: Divergence Equivalence (from $L$)
> For a sequence $X=(x_n)$ and $L\in\mathbb{R}$, the following are equivalent:
> 1. $x_n \not\to L$.
> 2. There exists $\epsilon_0>0$ such that for every $k\in\mathbb{N}$ there is an index $n_k\ge k$ with
>    $$
>    |x_{n_k} - L| \ge \epsilon_0.
>    $$
> 3. There exists $\epsilon_0>0$ and a subsequence $x_{n_k}$ of $X$ such that
>    $$
>    |x_{n_k} - L|\ge \epsilon_0\quad\text{for all }k\in\mathbb{N}.
>    $$

> [!pf] Proof
> - (3 ⇒ 1): If some subsequence stays at least $\epsilon_0$ away from $L$ for all $k$, then $x_n$ cannot converge to $L$ (by subsequence convergence theorem).
> - (2 ⇒ 3): Construct $(n_k)$ inductively.  
>   Choose $n_1\ge 1$ with $|x_{n_1}-L|\ge\epsilon_0$.  
>   Given $n_k$, choose $n_{k+1}\ge n_k+1$ with $|x_{n_{k+1}}-L|\ge\epsilon_0$.  
>   Then $(n_k)$ is strictly increasing, so $x_{n_k}$ is a subsequence satisfying (3).
> - (1 ⇒ 2): Negate the definition of convergence:
>   $$
>   \exists\,\epsilon_0>0\ \forall N\in\mathbb{N}\ \exists n\ge N\text{ with }|x_n-L|\ge \epsilon_0.
>   $$
>   Relabel $N=k$ and the corresponding $n$ as $n_k$ to obtain (2).


# Unbounded Sequences

## Definitions

> [!def] Definition: Bounded and Unbounded Sequences
> - Bounded: There exists $M>0$ such that $|x_n|\le M$ for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$.
> - Unbounded: For every $K\in\mathbb{N}$, there exists $n\in\mathbb{N}$ with $|x_n|>K$.
>   Equivalently, there exists a strictly increasing sequence $(n_k)$ with $|x_{n_k}|>k$ for all $k$.

## Reciprocal-Subsequence Tends to Zero

> [!thm] Theorem: Unbounded Implies Reciprocal-Subsequence Tends to Zero
> If $X=(x_n)$ is unbounded, then there exists a subsequence $(x_{n_k})$ such that
> $$
> \lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{x_{n_k}}=0.
> $$

> [!pf] Proof
> Since $X$ is unbounded, inductively choose $n_k$ such that $n_{k+1}\ge n_k+1$ and
> $$
> |x_{n_k}|\ge k\quad\text{for all }k\in\mathbb{N}.
> $$
> In particular, $x_{n_k}\neq 0$. Then
> $$
> \left|\frac{1}{x_{n_k}}\right|\le \frac{1}{k}\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}0,
> $$
> hence $\frac{1}{x_{n_k}}\to 0$.

> [!?] Question: Constructing the Subsequence
> Find a subsequence $(x_{n_k})$ with $|x_{n_k}|>k$ for all $k\in\mathbb{N}$.  
> Then
> $$
> 0<\left|\frac{1}{x_{n_k}}\right|<\frac{1}{k}\ \ \Longrightarrow\ \ \frac{1}{x_{n_k}}\to 0.
> $$


# Divergence Criteria

> [!thm] Theorem: Two Simple Divergence Tests
> A sequence $X=(x_n)$ diverges if any of the following holds:
> 1. $X$ has two convergent subsequences $x_{n_k}\to L_1$ and $x_{m_k}\to L_2$ with $L_1\ne L_2$.
> 2. $X$ is unbounded.


# Examples

> [!lem] Lemma: Limit of $b^n$ for $0<b<1$
> $$
> 0<b<1\ \Longrightarrow\ \lim_{n\to\infty} b^n=0.
> $$

> [!pf] Proof
> The sequence $x_n=b^n$ is decreasing and bounded below by $0$, hence has a limit $x\in[0,1)$ by the monotone convergence theorem.  
> The subsequence $x_{2n}=b^{2n}$ also converges to $x$. But $b^{2n}=(b^n)^2$, so by continuity of $t\mapsto t^2$,
> $$
> b^{2n}=(b^n)^2\ \longrightarrow\ x^2,
> $$
> and also $b^{2n}\to x$, hence $x=x^2$. Therefore $x\in\{0,1\}$. Since $x\le b<1$, we must have $x=0$.
