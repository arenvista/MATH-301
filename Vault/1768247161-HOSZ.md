## Characterization Theorem
**Theorem:** Let $S \subset \mathbb{R}$ contain at least two points. If $S$ possesses the property:
$$x, y \in S \land x < y \implies [x, y] \subset S$$
Then $S$ is an interval.

### Proof Strategy
To prove this, we divide the proof into 4 cases based on boundedness:
1.  **Bounded**
2.  **Bounded Above** (but not below)
3.  **Bounded Below** (but not above)
4.  **Unbounded** (neither above nor below)

---

### Case 1: $S$ is Bounded
$S$ is bounded both above and below.
Let $a = \inf S$ and $b = \sup S$.
By definition of inf/sup, we know that $S \subset [a, b]$.

**Goal:** We must prove that $(a, b) \subset S$.

1.  Let $z$ be any number such that $a < z < b$.
2.  Since $a < z$, $z$ is *not* a lower bound of $S$. Thus, $\exists ~x \in S$ such that $x < z$.
3.  Since $z < b$, $z$ is *not* an upper bound of $S$. Thus, $\exists ~y \in S$ such that $z < y$.
4.  We have established that $x < z < y$ where $x, y \in S$.
5.  By the hypothesis property, $[x, y] \subset S$. Since $z \in [x, y]$, then $z \in S$.

**Conclusion:** $(a, b) \subset S$.
(Depending on whether endpoints are included, $S$ is $(a, b)$, $[a, b]$, $[a, b)$, or $(a, b]$).

---

### Case 2: $S$ is Bounded Above
$S$ has an upper bound, but extends infinitely to the left.
Let $b = \sup S$. Then $S \subset (-\infty, b]$.

**Goal:** We must prove that $(-\infty, b) \subset S$.

1.  Let $z < b$.
2.  Since $z < b$, $\exists ~y \in S$ such that $z < y$ (definition of supremum).
3.  Since $S$ is not bounded below, no number is a lower bound. Thus, $\exists ~x \in S$ such that $x < z$.
4.  We have $x < z < y$ with $x, y \in S$.
5.  Therefore $z \in [x, y] \subset S$.

**Conclusion:** $(-\infty, b) \subset S$.

---

### Case 3: $S$ is Bounded Below
$S$ has a lower bound, but extends infinitely to the right.
Let $a = \inf S$. Then $S \subset [a, \infty)$.

**Goal:** We must prove that $(a, \infty) \subset S$.

1.  Let $z > a$.
2.  Since $z > a$, $\exists ~x \in S$ such that $x < z$ (definition of infimum).
3.  Since $S$ is not bounded above, no number is an upper bound. Thus, $\exists ~y \in S$ such that $y > z$.
4.  We have $x < z < y$ with $x, y \in S$.
5.  Therefore $z \in [x, y] \subset S$.

**Conclusion:** $(a, \infty) \subset S$.

---

### Case 4: $S$ is Unbounded
$S$ has neither an upper nor a lower bound.

**Goal:** We must prove that $\mathbb{R} \subset S$ (meaning $S = \mathbb{R}$).

1.  Let $z \in \mathbb{R}$.
2.  Since $S$ is not bounded below, $\exists ~x \in S$ such that $x < z$.
3.  Since $S$ is not bounded above, $\exists ~y \in S$ such that $y > z$.
4.  Therefore $z \in [x, y] \subset S \implies z \in S$.

**Conclusion:** $S = (-\infty, \infty)$.
