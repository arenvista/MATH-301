# For Quiz

## 1) Limsup/Liminf: Definitions and Basic Inequalities

> [!def] Limsup and liminf via tails
> Let $(x_n)$ be a real sequence and define the tail suprema and infima by
> $$s_n := \sup_{m\ge n} x_m, \qquad t_n := \inf_{m\ge n} x_m.$$
> Then
> $$\overline{\lim}_{n\to\infty} x_n \;=\; \lim_{n\to\infty} s_n, \qquad \underline{\lim}_{n\to\infty} x_n \;=\; \lim_{n\to\infty} t_n.$$

> [!lem] Tailwise subadditivity of sup
> For every fixed $n\in\mathbb{N}$ and real sequences $(x_n)$, $(y_n)$,
> $$\sup_{m\ge n}(x_m+y_m)\;\le\;\sup_{m\ge n} x_m\;+\;\sup_{m\ge n} y_m.$$
> Proof sketch: For all $m\ge n$, $x_m \le \sup_{k\ge n}x_k$ and $y_m \le \sup_{k\ge n}y_k$, hence add and take $\sup$ over $m\ge n$.

> [!thm] Subadditivity of limsup
> For real sequences $(x_n)$ and $(y_n)$,
> $$\limsup_{n\to\infty} (x_n+y_n)\;\le\;\limsup_{n\to\infty} x_n\;+\;\limsup_{n\to\infty} y_n.$$
> 
> > [!pf] Proof
> > Let $u_n:=\sup_{m\ge n}(x_m+y_m)$, $s_n:=\sup_{m\ge n}x_m$, $t_n:=\sup_{m\ge n}y_m$. By the lemma, $u_n\le s_n+t_n$ for all $n$. Passing to the limit,
> > $$\lim_{n\to\infty}u_n\;\le\;\lim_{n\to\infty}s_n+\lim_{n\to\infty}t_n,$$
> > i.e.,
> > $$\limsup_{n\to\infty}(x_n+y_n)\;\le\;\limsup_{n\to\infty}x_n+\limsup_{n\to\infty}y_n.$$

> [!lem] Liminf identities
> For any real sequence $(x_n)$,
> $$\underline{\lim}_{n\to\infty} x_n \;=\; -\,\overline{\lim}_{n\to\infty}(-x_n)
> \;=\; \lim_{n\to\infty} \inf_{m\ge n} x_m.$$
> 
> > [!pf] Proof
> > Using $-\sup A=\inf(-A)$, we compute
> > $$\underline{\lim} x_n
> > = -\overline{\lim}(-x_n)
> > = -\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{m\ge n}(-x_m)
> > = -\lim_{n\to\infty}\bigl(-\inf_{m\ge n}x_m\bigr)
> > = \lim_{n\to\infty}\inf_{m\ge n}x_m.$$

## 2) Convergence of p-series partial sums (special case p=2)

> [!thm] Convergence of $\sum 1/n^p$ for $p>1$ via integrals
> For $p>1$, define
> $$S_n(p):=\sum_{k=1}^n \frac{1}{k^p}.$$
> Then $(S_n(p))$ is increasing and bounded above by
> $$S_n(p)\;\le\;1+\int_1^\infty \frac{dx}{x^p} \;=\; 1+\frac{1}{p-1}.$$
> Hence $(S_n(p))$ converges.
> 
> > [!pf] Proof
> > Since the terms are positive, $S_n(p)$ is increasing. For $k\ge2$ the function $f(x)=x^{-p}$ is decreasing, so
> > $$\frac{1}{k^p}\;\le\;\int_{k-1}^{k}\frac{dx}{x^p}.$$
> > Summing from $k=2$ to $n$ gives
> > $$\sum_{k=2}^n \frac{1}{k^p} \;\le\; \int_1^n \frac{dx}{x^p}
> > \;=\;\left[\frac{x^{1-p}}{1-p}\right]_1^n
> > \;=\;\frac{1-n^{1-p}}{p-1}\;\le\;\frac{1}{p-1}.$$
> > Therefore $S_n(p)\le 1+\frac{1}{p-1}$, so $(S_n(p))$ is increasing and bounded, hence convergent.

> [!cor] Special case $p=2$
> For
> $$x_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k^2},$$
> we have $x_n\le 1+\int_1^\infty x^{-2}\,dx=2$, so $(x_n)$ converges. (Its limit is $\zeta(2)=\pi^2/6$, though this value is not needed for convergence.)

## 3) Monotone/Cauchy criteria for the iteration $x_{n+1}=\sqrt{2+x_n}$

> [!thm] Convergence and limit of $x_{n+1}=\sqrt{2+x_n}$
> Let $x_1\ge 0$ and define $x_{n+1}=\sqrt{2+x_n}$. Then $(x_n)$ converges to $2$.
> 
> - If $x_1\le 2$, then $(x_n)$ is monotone increasing and bounded above by $2$.
> - If $x_1\ge 2$, then $(x_n)$ is monotone decreasing and bounded below by $2$.
> 
> > [!pf] Proof (monotone + bounded)
> > 1) If $x_k\le x_{k+1}$, then $2+x_k\le 2+x_{k+1}$ and the monotonicity of $\sqrt{\cdot}$ on $[0,\infty)$ yields $x_{k+1}\le x_{k+2}$. With the base $x_1\le x_2$ when $x_1\le 2$, induction gives monotone increase.
> > 
> > 2) If $x_n\le 2$, then $x_{n+1}=\sqrt{2+x_n}\le \sqrt{4}=2$. Thus $x_n\le 2$ for all $n$, so the increasing sequence is bounded and convergent.
> > 
> > 3) Any limit $L$ satisfies $L=\sqrt{2+L}$, hence $L^2=L+2$, i.e.,
> > $$(L-2)(L+1)=0.$$
> > Because $x_n\ge 0$, the only admissible limit is $L=2$.
> > 
> > The case $x_1\ge 2$ is analogous: one checks $x_{n+1}\le x_n$ and $x_n\ge 2$, so $(x_n)$ decreases to $2$.

### Alternative: Cauchy (contraction) argument (for $x_1\ge 0$)

> [!pf] Contraction of successive differences
> For all $n$,
> $$|x_{n+2}-x_{n+1}|=\left|\sqrt{2+x_{n+1}}-\sqrt{2+x_n}\right|
> =\frac{|x_{n+1}-x_n|}{\sqrt{2+x_{n+1}}+\sqrt{2+x_n}}.$$
> If $x_n\ge 0$, then $\sqrt{2+x_n}\ge \sqrt{2}$, hence
> $$|x_{n+2}-x_{n+1}|\;\le\;\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\;|x_{n+1}-x_n|.$$
> Thus the successive differences decay geometrically, so $(x_n)$ is Cauchy and convergent; the limit must solve $L=\sqrt{2+L}$, hence $L=2$.

## 4) A divergent monotone sequence: $x_{n+1}=x_n+\frac{1}{x_n}$

> [!lem] Monotonicity
> If $x_1=a>0$ and $x_{n+1}=x_n+\frac{1}{x_n}$, then $x_{n+1}-x_n=\frac{1}{x_n}>0$, so $(x_n)$ is strictly increasing.

> [!thm] Divergence to $+\infty$
> The sequence $(x_n)$ is unbounded and
> $$x_n \;\ge\; \sqrt{a^2+2(n-1)}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x_n\to+\infty.$$
> 
> > [!pf] Proof
> > Compute the difference of squares:
> > $$x_{n+1}^2-x_n^2=(x_n+\tfrac{1}{x_n})^2-x_n^2
> > =2+\frac{1}{x_n^2}\;\ge\;2.$$
> > Summing from $1$ to $n-1$,
> > $$x_n^2\;\ge\;x_1^2+2(n-1)=a^2+2(n-1),$$
> > so $x_n\ge \sqrt{a^2+2(n-1)}\to\infty$.

Example (first few terms):
$$
\begin{aligned}
x_1&=a,\\
x_2&=a+\frac{1}{a},\\
x_3&=a+\frac{1}{a}+\frac{1}{a+\frac{1}{a}}
=a+\frac{1}{a}+\frac{a}{a^2+1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

## 5) Subsequence approaching the supremum

> [!thm] Subsequence converging to the supremum
> Let $(x_n)$ be a bounded real sequence and let $s=\sup\{x_n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}$. If $s\notin\{x_n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}$, then there exists a subsequence $(x_{n_k})$ such that $x_{n_k}\to s$.
> 
> - The indices can be chosen strictly increasing: $n_1<n_2<\cdots$.
> - The subsequence satisfies $s-\tfrac{1}{k}<x_{n_k}\le s$ for all $k$, hence $x_{n_k}\to s$.
> 
> > [!pf] Proof
> > For each $k\in\mathbb{N}$, the number $s-\tfrac{1}{k}$ is not an upper bound of $\{x_n\}$ (since $s$ is the least upper bound). Thus we can pick $n_k$ with $x_{n_k}>s-\tfrac{1}{k}$. Choose them inductively so that $n_{k+1}>n_k$. Then
> > $$s-\frac{1}{k}<x_{n_k}\le s \quad\text{for all }k,$$
> > and letting $k\to\infty$ gives $x_{n_k}\to s$ by the squeeze theorem.
> > If $s$ were attained (i.e., $x_{n_0}=s$ for some $n_0$), then the constant subsequence $x_{n_k}=s$ also converges to $s$.
