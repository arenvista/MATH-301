# Quiz

## Question 1
$$ x_1 = 1/4 \quad \quad x_{n+1} = x_n^2 \frac{1}{4} $$ 

### Method: Monotone
Looking at the first two terms shows the sequence is increasing:
$$
\begin{aligned}
x_2 = (1/4)^2+1/4 > 1/4 = x_1
\end{aligned}
$$ 

Then we must show that $\{ x_n \}$ is increasing via induction. 

Show $x_1 < x_2$ (Done above)

Suppose $x_k < x_{k+1}$. 

Observe
$$
\begin{gathered}
x_{k+1} := k_k + 1/4 \\
x_k \leq  1/2 \\
x_k^2 \leq  1/4 \\
x_k^2 1/4 \leq 1/2
\end{gathered}
$$ 

Therefore we have shown $x_k^2 < x_{k+1}^2$. Thus the sequence is increasing. 


Show that the sequence $\{ x_n \}$ is bounded above:

Base Case: $x_1 = 1/4 < 1/2$ 

Supposition: $x_k < 1/2$

Inductive:
$$
x_{k+1} := x_k^2 + 1/4 \\
$$ 

$$
\begin{gathered}
x_{n+1} = x_n^2 + 1/4 \quad x = x^2 + 1/4 \\
\implies x^2 - x + 1/4 = 0 \\
\equiv (x-1/2)^2 = 0
\end{gathered}
$$ 

### Method: Contractive
$$ |x_{n+1} - x_n| \leq  c |x_n - x_{n-1}| $$ 
Observe:
$$ |x_{n+1} - x_n| = |x_n^2 + 1/4 - x_{n-1}^2 - 1/4| = |x_n^2 - x_{n-1}^2| = \underset{<c}{x_n+x_{n+1}} |x_n - x_{n+1}| $$ 

### Analysis of the Inequality

The inequality suggests that

$$|x_{n+1} - x_n| \leq c\, |x_n - x_{n-1}|,$$

which at first appears similar to the definition of a contractive sequence.

However, for a sequence to be contractive, there must exist a constant \(c\) with \(0 < c < 1\), such that

$$|x_{n+1} - x_n| \leq c|x_n - x_{n-1}| \quad \text{for all } n.$$

In this case, when we expand the right-hand side, we get:

$$|x_n^2 - x_{n-1}^2| = |x_n + x_{n-1}|\,|x_n - x_{n-1}|,$$

so the supposed 'constant' \(c\) actually depends on \(n\) because it is \(|x_n + x_{n-1}|\), which could grow arbitrarily large.

Therefore, \(c\) is not a fixed number less than 1, and the sequence does **not** satisfy the definition of a contractive sequence.

## Question 3

Consider the sequence $\{ x_n \}$ defined by:
$$ x_n = 1 + (-1)^n \frac{n^2}{n^2 -n + 1} $$ 


Note that:
$$
\begin{gathered}
x_{2n} = 1 + \frac{ (2n)^2}{(2n)^2 - 2n + 1} \quad \lim_{n \to \infty} x_{2n} = 2 \\
x_{2n+1} = 1 + \frac{ (2n)^2}{(2n)^2 - 2n + 1}  \quad \lim_{n \to \infty} x_{2n+1} = 0 \\
\end{gathered}
$$ 

So by divergence criterion the sequence $\{ x_n \}$ does not converge and $\underline{\lim} (x_n) = 0$

# Continous Functions on Intervals 

> [!def] ## Bounded Functions
> A function from $f: A \rightarrow R$ is said to be ***bounded*** on $A$ if there exists a constant $M > 0$ such that:
$$ ~\forall~ x \in A,~~ |f(x)| \leq M $$


> [!def] ## Unbounded Functions
A function from $f: A \rightarrow R$ is said to be ***NOT bounded*** on $A$ if there exists a sequence $(x_n) \in A$ such that:
$$ \lim_{n \to \infty} |f(x_n)|  = \infty $$ 

## Bounded Theorem
> [!thm] 
> A function $f$ is unbounded on $I$ If we can say the following:
> 1. $I = [a,b]$ be a closed and bounded interval of $R$.
> 2. $f: I \rightarrow R$ be continous on $I$

### Proof: Bounded Theorem
If $f: A \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be a function that is not bounded on $I$. Then $\exists~ \{ x_n \} \in I$ such that $\lim_{n \to \infty} f(x_n) = \infty$.

Since $I$ is bounded and $\{ x_n \} \in I$. This means that $\{ x_n \}$ must also be bounded.

By Bolzano Weisterstrases given a bounded $\{ x_n \}$ that is bounded. There must be some subsequence $\{ x_k \} \in  \{ x_n \}$ such that $\lim_{k \to \infty} x_k \rightarrow x^*$  where $x^* \in I$ since $I$ is closed. 

Since $f$ is continous on $I$ and $x^* \in I$ f is continous at $x^*$

By the sequential criterion of continunity, $\lim_{k \to \infty} f(x_{n_k}) = f(x^*)$

As $\{ x_{n_k} \} \subseteq \{ x_n \}$:
$$
\begin{cases}
\lim_{k \to \infty} f(x_{n_k}) = f(x^*) \\
\lim_{n \to \infty} f(x_n) = \infty
\end{cases}
$$ 

But these would imply that $f(x^*) = \infty$. However, as $f: A \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ this means that $f(x^*)$ should be in $\mathbb{R}$. As $\infty \not\in \mathbb{R}$ this must be a contradiction $\unicode{x21af}$.

#### Example
$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) & = 1/x \quad \text{on} (0,1] \\
f(x) & = x \quad \text{on} [0,\infty) \\
f(x) & = 
\begin{cases}
\frac{1}{|x|} &  x \in [-1,1]-\{ 0 \} \\
0 &  x=0
\end{cases} \\
\therefore ~f(x): [-1,1] &  \rightarrow \mathbb{R}, \text{ but $f$ is not continous at $0$}
\end{aligned}
$$ 

# Definition: Min-Max 
Let $f: A \rightarrow R$, $f$ has an absolute maximum/minimum on $A$ if there exists a point $x^* \in A$ such that:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\forall~ x \in A,~~ f(x) \leq  f(x^*)  \\
\forall~ x \in A,~~ f(x) \geq   f(x_*)  \\
\end{gathered}
$$ 

> [!cor] Relation to Supremem and Infimum
> Observe how these statements relate to supremem and infimum
> - $\forall~ x \in A,~~ f(x) \leq  f(x^*)$ really is saying $f(x^*) = \sup f(A)$
> - $\forall~ x \in A,~~ f(x) \geq   f(x_*)$ is really saying $f(x_*) = \inf f(A)$
> Naturally then, this means that the supremem and infimum of $f(A)$ must exist.

## Min-Max Theorem
If $\exists~ I := [a,b]$ where $I$ is a closed and bounded interval. and $f: I \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be continous on $I$. Then $f$ has an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum on $I$

### Proof: Min-Max Theorem

By the Bounded Theorem, $f(I)$ is bounded.
By the completeness preoperty, $\exists~ \sup f(I), \inf f(I) \in \mathbb{R}$


To prove this theorem we need to fin $x^*$ and $x_*$ in $I$ such that:
$$
\begin{gathered}
f(x^*) = \sup f(I) \\
f(x_*) = \inf f(I)
\end{gathered}
$$ 

$$
\begin{gathered}
\forall~ n \in \mathbb{N}, \exists~ x_n \in I ~s.t.~ \\
\sup f(I) - \frac{1}{n} \leq f(x_n) \leq  \sup f(I)
\end{gathered}
$$ 

Therefore $\lim_{n \to \infty} f(x_n) = \sup f(I)$. 

So $\exists~ \{ x_{n_k} \} \subseteq \{ x_n \}$  such that $\lim_{k \to \infty} x_{n_k}  = x^* \in I$. 

By sequential criterion of continunity $f(x_{n_k}) \rightarrow f(x^*)$ as $k \rightarrow \infty$

As the main sequence $\lim_{n \to \infty} \{ x_n \} = \sup f(I)$ then the subsequence $x_{n_k}$ must also converge to the same limit. Thus $f(x^*) = \sup f(I)$
