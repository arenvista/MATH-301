z# p-Series Partial Sums

## Recall: Harmonic Series Diverges

For the harmonic series partial sums
$$
x_n = \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{1}{k},
$$
we have $x_n \to \infty$ (diverges).

> [!thm] p-Series Test (Partial Sums)
> For $p>1$, the sequence of partial sums
> $$
> x_n(p) = \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{1}{k^p}
> $$
> is convergent. For $p \le 1$, it is divergent.

### Proof 1: Integral Comparison + Monotone Convergence

> [!pf] Proof
> - Monotonicity: Since all terms $1/k^p>0$, the partial sums $x_n(p)$ are increasing.
> - Boundedness: For the decreasing function $f(x)=x^{-p}$ and $p>1$, the integral test inequalities give
>   $$
>   \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{1}{k^p} \le \frac{1}{1^p} + \int_1^n \frac{dx}{x^p} \le 1 + \int_1^\infty \frac{dx}{x^p}.
>   $$
>   Compute
>   $$
>   \int_1^\infty x^{-p}\,dx
>   = \left[\frac{x^{1-p}}{1-p}\right]_1^\infty
>   = \frac{1}{p-1}.
>   $$
>   Hence $x_n(p) \le 1 + \frac{1}{p-1}$, so $x_n(p)$ is bounded above.
> - By the Monotone Convergence Theorem (for sequences), an increasing and bounded sequence converges.

### Proof 2: Cauchy Criterion via Integral Tail Bound

> [!?] Why the naive bound fails
> A tempting (but wrong) step is
> $$
> \sum_{k=m+1}^n \frac{1}{k^p}
> \le \left(\frac{1}{(m+3)^p}\right)\cdot m.
> $$
> This fails because:
> - The number of terms is $n-m$ (not $m$), and it depends on $n$.
> - Since $1/k^p$ is decreasing, $\frac{1}{(m+3)^p}$ is a smaller term than most terms in the sum, so multiplying it by the number of terms underestimates the sum, not overestimates it. For an upper bound with a decreasing sequence, you would replace each term by a larger earlier term (e.g., $\frac{1}{(m+1)^p}$), which still leaves a factor of $(n-m)$ depending on $n$ and does not yield a Cauchy bound independent of $n$.
> 
> The correct approach uses an integral tail bound that depends only on $m$.

> [!pf] Proof (Cauchy)
> For $m<n$,
> $$
> \bigl|x_n(p)-x_m(p)\bigr|
> = \sum_{k=m+1}^n \frac{1}{k^p}
> \le \int_m^n \frac{dx}{x^p}
> \le \int_m^\infty \frac{dx}{x^p}
> = \frac{m^{1-p}}{p-1}.
> $$
> Given $\epsilon>0$, choose $M$ so that $\dfrac{M^{1-p}}{p-1}<\epsilon$ (possible since $p>1$). Then for all $m\ge M$ and all $n>m$, we have $\lvert x_n(p)-x_m(p)\rvert<\epsilon$. Thus $(x_n(p))$ is Cauchy and hence convergent.

### Monotonicity in the Exponent

> [!lem] Partial-sum ordering in $p$
> If $0<p<q$, then for every $n$,
> $$
> \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{1}{k^p} \ge \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{1}{k^q}.
> $$
> In particular,
> $$
> \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{1}{k^{p<1}} \ge \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{1}{k} \ge \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{1}{k^{p>1}}.
> $$
> This comparison alone does not prove convergence/divergence, but it shows the relative size of partial sums as $p$ varies.


# Properly Divergent Sequences

> [!imp] Monotonicity vs. “tending to infinity”
> A sequence may tend to $+\infty$ without being monotone. For example, $x_n = n + (-1)^n$ is not monotone, yet $\lim_{n\to\infty} x_n = \infty$.

## Definitions

> [!def] Proper divergence
> Let $(x_n)$ be a real sequence.
> - $x_n \to \infty$ if for every $\alpha>0$ there exists $N$ such that $n\ge N \Rightarrow x_n>\alpha$.
> - $x_n \to -\infty$ if $-x_n \to \infty$.
> - $(x_n)$ is properly divergent if $x_n \to \infty$ or $x_n \to -\infty$.

### Example: Exponential Growth

> [!thm] Exponential sequences diverge
> If $c>1$, then $c^n \to \infty$.
>
> [!pf] Proof
> Write $c=1+a$ with $a>0$. By Bernoulli’s inequality, $(1+a)^n \ge 1+na > na$. Given any $\alpha>0$, choose $n>\alpha/a$ to get $c^n>(1+a)^n>na>\alpha$.

> [!cor] Reciprocal tends to zero
> If $x_n \to \infty$ or $x_n \to -\infty$ and $x_n\ne 0$ eventually, then $\dfrac{1}{x_n}\to 0$.
> The converse need not hold: $x_n=(-1)^n n$ satisfies $1/x_n \to 0$ but is not properly divergent (it oscillates unboundedly).

## Divergence Tests

> [!thm] Divergence of monotone sequences
> A monotone sequence is properly divergent if and only if it is unbounded.
>
> [!pf] Proof
> - If monotone increasing and unbounded, then for any $\alpha$ there exists $n$ with $x_n>\alpha$. Monotonicity gives $x_k\ge x_n>\alpha$ for all $k\ge n$, hence $x_k\to\infty$.
> - If monotone decreasing and unbounded below, then $x_k\to -\infty$.
> - The converse is immediate: if a monotone sequence tends to $\pm\infty$, it is unbounded.

> [!thm] Direct comparison (divergent form)
> Suppose for all sufficiently large $n$, $x_n \le y_n$.
> - If $x_n \to \infty$, then $y_n \to \infty$.
> - If $y_n \to -\infty$, then $x_n \to -\infty$.
>
> [!pf] Proof
> Fix $\alpha>0$. If $x_n\to\infty$, then for large $n$, $x_n>\alpha$, hence $y_n\ge x_n>\alpha$, so $y_n\to\infty$. The other case is similar.

> [!thm] Limit comparison (divergent form)
> If $\displaystyle \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_n}{y_n}=L$ with $L>0$, then $x_n\to\infty$ iff $y_n\to\infty$ (and similarly for $-\infty$ when consistent with signs).
>
> [!pf] Proof
> For $\epsilon=\tfrac{L}{2}$, there exists $N$ such that for $n\ge N$,
> $$
> \frac{L}{2}<\frac{x_n}{y_n}<\frac{3L}{2}.
> $$
> Thus $\frac{L}{2}y_n < x_n < \frac{3L}{2}y_n$. If $y_n\to\infty$, the left inequality forces $x_n\to\infty$. If $x_n\to\infty$, the right inequality forces $y_n\to\infty$.


# Limits of Functions

## Domain and Cluster Points

Consider $f$ defined on
$$
A=(0,\infty)\cup\{-1\},\quad
f(x)=\begin{cases}
1/x,& x>0,\\
1,& x=-1.
\end{cases}
$$

> [!?] Can we define $\lim_{x\to -\tfrac{1}{2}} f(x)$?
> No. The point $c=-\tfrac12$ is not a cluster point of the domain $A$, so the limit at $c$ is not defined.

> [!def] Cluster point
> Let $A\subseteq\mathbb{R}$. A point $c\in\mathbb{R}$ is a cluster point of $A$ if
> $$
> \forall\,\delta>0,\ \exists\,x\in A\setminus\{c\}\ \text{such that}\ |x-c|<\delta.
> $$

Examples:
- If $A$ is finite, it has no cluster points.
- $A=\{n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}$ has no cluster points in $\mathbb{R}$.
- $A=\{1/n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}$ has a single cluster point $0$.
- $A=(0,1)$ has cluster points $[0,1]$.
- For $A=(0,\infty)\cup\{-1\}$, the cluster points are $[0,\infty)$; in particular, $-1$ is isolated and not a cluster point.

> [!thm] Sequential characterization of cluster points
> A point $c\in\mathbb{R}$ is a cluster point of $A\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ iff there exists a sequence $(a_n)\subset A\setminus\{c\}$ with $a_n\to c$.
>
> [!pf] Proof sketch
> If $c$ is a cluster point, choose $a_n\in A\setminus\{c\}$ with $|a_n-c|<1/n$. Conversely, if such a sequence exists, then every neighborhood of $c$ contains infinitely many $a_n\in A\setminus\{c\}$.

## Limit of a Function at a Cluster Point

> [!def] Limit at a cluster point
> Let $A\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ and let $c$ be a cluster point of $A$. A number $L$ is the limit of $f:A\to\mathbb{R}$ at $c$ if
> $$
> \forall\,\epsilon>0,\ \exists\,\delta>0\ \text{such that}\ \forall\,x\in A,\ 0<|x-c|<\delta\ \Rightarrow\ |f(x)-L|<\epsilon.
> $$
> We write $\lim_{x\to c} f(x)=L$.

### Example: $\lim_{x\to c}\frac{1}{x}=\frac{1}{c}$ for $c\ne 0$

> [!thm] Continuity of $x\mapsto 1/x$ away from 0
> If $c\ne 0$ and $A\subseteq\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$ has $c$ as a cluster point, then
> $$
> \lim_{x\to c} \frac{1}{x}=\frac{1}{c}.
> $$
>
> [!pf] Proof
> For $x\ne 0$,
> $$
> \left|\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{c}\right|=\frac{|x-c|}{|xc|}.
> $$
> Choose
> $$
> \delta=\min\left\{\frac{|c|}{2},\ \frac{|c|^2\,\epsilon}{2}\right\}.
> $$
> If $0<|x-c|<\delta\le \frac{|c|}{2}$, then $|x|\ge |c|/2$, hence
> $$
> \left|\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{c}\right|
> =\frac{|x-c|}{|xc|}
> \le \frac{|x-c|}{(|c|/2)\,|c|}
> =\frac{2}{|c|^2}|x-c|
> < \frac{2}{|c|^2}\cdot \frac{|c|^2\epsilon}{2}
> =\epsilon.
> $$
> Therefore, $\lim_{x\to c} 1/x = 1/c$.
