# Section 1.3 Finite and Infinite Sets
> [!def] Countable Set
> A set $S$ is said to have $n$ elements if there exists a bijection from $\{ 1,2,3,...,n \}$ onto $S$

> [!def] Denumerable
> A set $S$ is said to be denumerable if it is countable and infinite


> [!thm] Properties of Countable Sets Intersections/Unions
> Suppose $S$ and $T$ are sets 
> - If one of $S$ or $T$ is countable. Then $S \cap T$ is countable
> - If one of $S$ and $T$ are both countable. Then $S \cup T$ is countable.
>
> > [!thm] Constructing a Bijection on Denumerable Set Unions
> > Suppose $S,T$ are denumerable.
> > - $f:\mathbb{N} \rightarrow S$
> > - $g:\mathbb{N} \rightarrow T$
> >
> > Observe we can not construct a bijection $h: \mathbb{N} \rightarrow S \cup T$ as we can send to both $S,T$ for some $n \in \mathbb{N}$.
> >
> > Thus we say $S \cup T-S$. We can express this as the following functions:
> > - $f: \mathbb{N} \rightarrow S$
> > - $g: \mathbb{N} \rightarrow T-S$
> > This allows us to create the bijective function:
> > $$
> > h(n) =
> > \begin{cases}
> > f(k) \quad \text{If } n=2k \\
> > g(k) \quad \text{If } n=2k+1
> > \end{cases}
> > $$
> > Observe the sequence:
> > $$
> > \begin{align}
> > h(1) & = g(1) \\
> > h(2) & = f(1) \\
> > h(3) & = g(2) \\
> > h(4) & = f(2)
> > \end{align}
> > $$
> >
> > > [!thm] Countable Set Implications
> > > The following statements are equivalent:
> > > - $S$ is countable
> > > - There exists a surjection of $\mathbb{N}$ onto $S$
> > > - There exists an injection of $S$ into $\mathbb{N}$
> > >
> > > Now notice:
> > > $$
> > > h(n) = 
> > > \begin{cases}
> > > f(k) \quad \text{If } n=2k \\
> > > g(k) \quad \text{If } n=2k+1
> > > \end{cases}
> > > $$
> > > Clearly covers all elements of in $S \cup T$
> > > 
> > > **Proposition:** Consider $\mathbb{Q}$, the set of all rational numbers. I propose $\mathbb{Q}$ is contable.
> > > Consider $\mathbb{Q}^+$ to be all positive rationals, and  $\mathbb{Q}^-$ to be all negative rationals:
> > > - Thus we can say: $\mathbb{Q} = \mathbb{Q}^+ \cup \{ 0 \} \cup \mathbb{Q}^-$
> > > More formally:
> > > - $\mathbb{Q}^+ = \{  \frac{m}{n}, m,n \in \mathbb{N} \}$
> > > 
> > > Consider the matrix of $\mathbb{Q}^+$:
> > > 
> > > | 1/1 | 2/1 | 3/1 | 4/1 | ... |
> > > |-----|-----|-----|-----|-----|
> > > | 1/2 | 2/2 | 3/2 | 4/2 | ... |
> > > | 1/3 | 2/3 | 3/3 | 4/3 | ... |
> > > | ... | ... | ... | ... | ... |
> > > 
> > > This clearly shows (by diagonals) that there exists a bijection $\mathbb{N} \rightarrow \mathbb{Q}^+$.
> > > 
> > > As $\mathbb{Q}^+$ and $\mathbb{Q}^-$ are inverses. We can assume that $\mathbb{Q}^-$ also follows the same properties.
> > >

# Section 2.1 Real Numbers
 
> [!def]
> ## Algebraic Properties of $\mathbb{R}$
> Two binary operations are denoted by "+" and "$\cdot$" are defined on $\mathbb{R}$ (addition and multiplication).
>
> > [!cor]
> > "-" is just the addition of inverses. 
> > "/" is just the multiplication of inveres.
>
> ### Addition
> 1. $a+b = b+a$
> 2. Associativity: $(a+1)+c = a+(b+c)$
> 3. Zero Property: there exists a $0 \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $a+0=a$
> 4. Existance of inverse: $\forall ~a \in \mathbb{R}, \exists ~-a \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $a+(-a)=0$ 
> ### Multiplication
> 1. $a \cdot b = b \cdot a$ 
> 2. $(a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c)$
> 3. $\exists ~ 1 \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $a \cdot 1 = a$
> 4. Existance of an inverse: $\forall ~a \neq 0, \exists ~\frac{1}{a} \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $a \cdot \frac{1}{a} = 1$
> ### Both
> 1. $a \cdot (b+c) = a \cdot + a \cdot c$

> [!pf] Example 1
> **Prove:** If $z,a \in \mathbb{R}$ with $z+a=a$ then $z=0$
> > [!cor]
> > *Corollary:* In multiplication it is akin to the statement, if $a*b=a*c$ and $a \neq b$ then $b=c$
> > Suppose: $z+a=a$ 
> > Observe:
>
> $$
> \begin{align}
> z+a & = a \\
> z+a + (-a) & = a + (-a) &&\text{ add -a by existance of inverse}\\
> z+(a + (-a)) & = (a + (-a)) &&\text{ rearange by association property}\\
> z+(a + (-a)) & = (a + (-a)) &&\text{ rearange by association property}\\
> z+(a + (-a)) & = (a + (-a)) = z + 0 &&\text{ continuation}\\
> z+0 & = z &&\text{ by existance of zero element }
> \end{align}
> $$
>
> > [!cor] Example 2
> > For "if $a*b=a*c$ and $a \neq b$ then $b=c$"
> > $$
> > \begin{align}
> > a*b &= a*c \\
> > (a*b)*\frac{1}{a} &= (a*c)*\frac{1}{a} \\
> > (a*\frac{1}{a}) * b &= (a*\frac{1}{a})*c \\
> > \text{Since } a*\frac{1}{a}&=1 \implies \\
> > 1*b&=1*c \\
> > 1*b&=b \land 1*c=c \implies b=c 
> > \end{align}
> > $$
>
> > [!pf] Example 3
> > **Prove:** $a*0=0$
> > We want to show $a*0+a=a$
> > Note:
> > - $a*0+a = a*0 + a*1$ by (m3).
> > - $a*0 + a*1 = a(0+1)$ by distribution law
> > - $a*1=1$ by properties of zero element (addition)
> > So by *Ex 1* we have $a*0=0$
> >  
> > > [!pf] Example 4
> > > **Prove:** If $a*b=c$. Then $a=0$ or $b=0$
> > > > Note: If $a=0$ then proven
> > >
> > > If $a \neq 0$. ***(Then our goal is to show $b=0$)***
> > > Since $a \neq 0$ by M4 we can say $\exists ~\frac{1}{a} \in \mathbb{R}$ s.t. $a*\frac{1}{a}=1$
> > > Since $a*b=0, \frac{1}{a}*(a*b)=\frac{1}{a}*0$
> > > By *Example 3*, $\frac{1}{a}*0=0$
> > > By M2, $\frac{1}{a}(c*b)=(\frac{1}{a}*a)*b=1*b$
> > > By M3, $1*b=b$
> > > Then, $\frac{1}{a}*(a*b)=b$ Thus $b=0$

## Rational Numbers $\mathbb{Q}$

> [!def]
> Rational Numbers are defined as:
> $\mathbb{Q} = \{ \frac{m}{n}, m,n \in \mathbb{Z} \}$

***How to show irrational numbers exist?***

> [!thm] Irrational Numbers
> $$\not \exists ~r \in \mathbb{Q} \text{ such that } r^2 = 2$$
> 
> > [!pf] Prove by contradiction. 
> > Suppose for the sake of contradiction $\exists ~r \in \mathbb{Q} \text{ such that } r^2 = 2$.
> > If $r \in \mathbb{Q}$. Then:
> > $$
> > r^2 = \frac{m}{n}*\frac{m}{n} = 2 
> > $$
> > Where for $m,n$ there is no common integer factor other than $1$.
> > By algebra:
> > $$
> > \frac{m}{n}*\frac{m}{n} = \frac{m^2}{n^2} = 2
> > $$
> > Thus
> > $$
> > 2n^2 = m^2
> > $$
> > Since $m^2$ is even. Then $m$ must also be even. 
> > Observe if $m$ is even then, $\exists ~k \in \mathbb{Z}$ s.t. $m=2k$
> > By subsitution:
> > $$
> > \begin{align}
> > 2n^2 &= m^2 \\
> > 2n^2 &= (2k)^2 \\
> > 2n^2 &= 4k^2 \\
> > n^2 &= 2k^2 \\
> > \end{align}
> > $$
> > If $m,n$ are even then there is a common integer factor $2$. Which is a contradiction. Thus we must take the original proposition.
> > $$\not \exists ~r \in \mathbb{Q} \text{ such that } r^2 = 2$$
> > 

## Ordering Properties of $\mathbb{R}$

> [!def]
> Let $P \neq \emptyset, P \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ called the set of positive set of real numbers. 
>  
> **Note $P$ retains the following properties:**
> 1. If $a,b \in P$ then $a+b \in P$
> 2. If $a,b \in P$ then $a*b \in P$
> 3. If $a \in \mathbb{R}$ then exactly one of the following must be true:
>     - $a \in P$
>     - $a \in 0$
>     - $-a \in P$
> Thus:
> - If $a \in P$ then we say $a$ is positive, written as $a>0$
> - If $-a \in P$ then we say $a$ is negative, written as $a<0$
> - If $a \in P \cup \{ 0 \}$ we say $a$ is non-negative $a \geq 0$
> - If $-a \in P \cup \{ 0 \}$ we say $a$ is non-positive $a \leq 0$
> 
> > [!def]
> > If $a-b \in P$ then we write $a>b$ or $b<a$
> > If $a-b \in P \cup \{ 0 \}$ then we write $a \geq b$ or $b \leq a$
> > So for any two real numbers $a,b$ exactly one of the following will hold:
> > - $a>b$
> > - $a<b$
> > - $a=b$
