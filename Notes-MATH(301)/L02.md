# Section 1.3: Finite and Infinite Sets

> [!def] Countable Set
> A set $S$ is said to have $n$ elements if there exists a **bijection** from $\{ 1, 2, 3, \dots, n \}$ onto $S$.

> [!def] Denumerable
> A set $S$ is said to be **denumerable** if it is countable and infinite.

> [!thm] Properties of Countable Sets (Intersections/Unions)
> Suppose $S$ and $T$ are sets:
> * If one of $S$ or $T$ is countable, then $S \cap T$ is countable.
> * If **both** $S$ and $T$ are countable, then $S \cup T$ is countable.
>
> > [!thm] Constructing a Bijection on Denumerable Set Unions
> > Suppose $S, T$ are denumerable.
> > * $f:\mathbb{N} \rightarrow S$
> > * $g:\mathbb{N} \rightarrow T$
> >
> > Observe we cannot simply construct a bijection $h: \mathbb{N} \rightarrow S \cup T$ because the sets might overlap (we could map to the same element twice).
> >
> > Thus, we consider $S \cup (T \setminus S)$. We can express this using the following functions:
> > * $f: \mathbb{N} \rightarrow S$
> > * $g: \mathbb{N} \rightarrow T \setminus S$
> >
> > This allows us to create the bijective function:
> > $$
> > h(n) =
> > \begin{cases}
> > f(k) \quad \text{If } n=2k \text{ (even)} \\
> > g(k) \quad \text{If } n=2k-1 \text{ (odd)}
> > \end{cases}
> > $$
> > *(Note: Adjusted indices slightly for standard mapping)*. Observe the sequence:
> > $$
> > \begin{align}
> > h(1) & = g(1) \\
> > h(2) & = f(1) \\
> > h(3) & = g(2) \\
> > h(4) & = f(2)
> > \end{align}
> > $$
> >
> > > [!thm] Countable Set Implications
> > > The following statements are equivalent:
> > > * $S$ is countable.
> > > * There exists a surjection of $\mathbb{N}$ onto $S$.
> > > * There exists an injection of $S$ into $\mathbb{N}$.
> > >
> > > **Proposition:** Consider $\mathbb{Q}$, the set of all rational numbers. I propose $\mathbb{Q}$ is countable.
> > >
> > > Consider $\mathbb{Q}^+$ to be all positive rationals, and $\mathbb{Q}^-$ to be all negative rationals:
> > > * Thus we can say: $\mathbb{Q} = \mathbb{Q}^+ \cup \{ 0 \} \cup \mathbb{Q}^-$
> > >
> > > More formally: $\mathbb{Q}^+ = \{ \frac{m}{n} \mid m,n \in \mathbb{N} \}$
> > >
> > > Consider the matrix of $\mathbb{Q}^+$:
> > >
> > > | | | | | |
> > > |---|---|---|---|---|
> > > | 1/1 | 2/1 | 3/1 | 4/1 | ... |
> > > | 1/2 | 2/2 | 3/2 | 4/2 | ... |
> > > | 1/3 | 2/3 | 3/3 | 4/3 | ... |
> > > | ... | ... | ... | ... | ... |
> > >
> > > This clearly shows (by traversing diagonals) that there exists a bijection $\mathbb{N} \rightarrow \mathbb{Q}^+$.
> > > As $\mathbb{Q}^+$ and $\mathbb{Q}^-$ are inverses, we can assume that $\mathbb{Q}^-$ follows the same properties.
> > >
> > > > [!thm] Union of Countable Sets
> > > > If $A_n$ is countable for each $n$, then $\bigcup^\infty_{n=1}A_n$ is countable.
> > > > $A_n$ is countable implies $\exists ~f^n: \mathbb{N} \rightarrow A_n$.
> > > >
> > > > * $A_1$: $f^1(1), f^1(2), f^1(3), \dots, f^1(n)$
> > > > * $A_2$: $f^2(1), f^2(2), f^2(3), \dots, f^2(n)$
> > > > * $A_n$: $f^n(1), f^n(2), f^n(3), \dots, f^n(n)$

# Section 2.1: Real Numbers

> [!def] Algebraic Properties of $\mathbb{R}$
> Two binary operations denoted by "+" and "$\cdot$" are defined on $\mathbb{R}$ (addition and multiplication).
>
> > [!cor] Note
> > * "$-$" is just the addition of inverses.
> > * "$/$" is just the multiplication of inverses.
>
> ### Addition
1.  **Commutativity:** $a+b = b+a$
2.  **Associativity:** $(a+b)+c = a+(b+c)$
3.  **Zero Property:** There exists a $0 \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $a+0=a$
4.  **Existence of inverse:** $\forall ~a \in \mathbb{R}, \exists ~-a \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $a+(-a)=0$
>
> ### Multiplication
1.  **Commutativity:** $a \cdot b = b \cdot a$
2.  **Associativity:** $(a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c)$
3.  **Identity:** $\exists ~ 1 \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $a \cdot 1 = a$
4.  **Existence of inverse:** $\forall ~a \neq 0, \exists ~\frac{1}{a} \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $a \cdot \frac{1}{a} = 1$
>
> ### Distributive Property
> 1.  $a \cdot (b+c) = a \cdot b + a \cdot c$

> [!pf] Example 1
> **Prove:** If $z,a \in \mathbb{R}$ with $z+a=a$, then $z=0$.
>
> > [!cor] Corollary
> > In multiplication, this is akin to the statement: if $a \cdot b = a \cdot c$ and $a \neq 0$, then $b=c$.
>
> **Proof:**
> Suppose $z+a=a$. Observe:
> $$
> \begin{align}
> z+a & = a \\
> (z+a) + (-a) & = a + (-a) &&\text{Add -a by existence of inverse}\\
> z+(a + (-a)) & = a + (-a) &&\text{Rearrange by associativity}\\
> z+0 & = 0 &&\text{By inverse property}\\
> z & = 0 &&\text{By existence of zero element}
> \end{align}
> $$
>
> > [!cor] Example 2
> > For "if $a \cdot b = a \cdot c$ and $a \neq 0$ then $b=c$":
> > $$
> > \begin{align}
> > a \cdot b &= a \cdot c \\
> > (a \cdot b) \cdot \frac{1}{a} &= (a \cdot c) \cdot \frac{1}{a} \\
> > (a \cdot \frac{1}{a}) \cdot b &= (a \cdot \frac{1}{a}) \cdot c \\
> > \text{Since } a \cdot \frac{1}{a} &= 1 \implies \\
> > 1 \cdot b &= 1 \cdot c \\
> > b &= c
> > \end{align}
> > $$
>
> > [!pf] Example 3
> > **Prove:** $a \cdot 0 = 0$
> >
> > We want to show $a \cdot 0 + a = a$.
> > Note:
> > * $a \cdot 0 + a = a \cdot 0 + a \cdot 1$ by (M3).
> > * $a \cdot 0 + a \cdot 1 = a(0+1)$ by distribution law.
> > * $a(0+1) = a \cdot 1 = a$ by properties of zero element (addition).
> >
> > So by *Ex 1* we have $a \cdot 0 = 0$.
> >
> > > [!pf] Example 4 (Zero Product Property)
> > > **Prove:** If $a \cdot b = 0$, then $a=0$ or $b=0$.
> > >
> > > *Note: If $a=0$ then proven.*
> > >
> > > **Case: If $a \neq 0$** (Then our goal is to show $b=0$)
> > > Since $a \neq 0$, by M4 we can say $\exists ~\frac{1}{a} \in \mathbb{R}$ s.t. $a \cdot \frac{1}{a}=1$.
> > >
> > > $$
> > > \begin{align}
> > > a \cdot b &= 0 \\
> > > \frac{1}{a} \cdot (a \cdot b) &= \frac{1}{a} \cdot 0 \\
> > > (\frac{1}{a} \cdot a) \cdot b &= 0 && \text{By Ex 3} \\
> > > 1 \cdot b &= 0 && \text{By M2 and M4} \\
> > > b &= 0 && \text{By M3}
> > > \end{align}
> > > $$
> > > Thus $b=0$.

## Rational Numbers $\mathbb{Q}$

> [!def] Rational Numbers
> Rational Numbers are defined as:
> $$\mathbb{Q} = \left\{ \frac{m}{n} \;\middle|\; m,n \in \mathbb{Z}, n \neq 0 \right\}$$

***How to show irrational numbers exist?***

> [!thm] Irrational Numbers
> There does not exist $r \in \mathbb{Q}$ such that $r^2 = 2$.
> $$\nexists ~r \in \mathbb{Q} \text{ such that } r^2 = 2$$
>
> > [!pf] Proof by contradiction
> > Suppose for the sake of contradiction $\exists ~r \in \mathbb{Q}$ such that $r^2 = 2$.
> >
> > If $r \in \mathbb{Q}$, then $r = \frac{m}{n}$ where $m,n$ share no common integer factor other than $1$ (irreducible fraction).
> >
> > $$
> > \begin{align}
> > r^2 = \left(\frac{m}{n}\right)^2 &= 2 \\
> > \frac{m^2}{n^2} &= 2 \\
> > m^2 &= 2n^2
> > \end{align}
> > $$
> >
> > Since $m^2$ is even ($2 \cdot \text{integer}$), $m$ must also be even.
> > If $m$ is even, $\exists ~k \in \mathbb{Z}$ s.t. $m=2k$.
> >
> > By substitution:
> > $$
> > \begin{align}
> > 2n^2 &= m^2 \\
> > 2n^2 &= (2k)^2 \\
> > 2n^2 &= 4k^2 \\
> > n^2 &= 2k^2
> > \end{align}
> > $$
> >
> > This implies $n^2$ is even, and thus $n$ is even.
> > If both $m$ and $n$ are even, they share a common factor of $2$. This contradicts the assumption that $\frac{m}{n}$ is in lowest terms.
> >
> > Thus, we must accept the original proposition: **$\sqrt{2}$ is irrational.**

## Ordering Properties of $\mathbb{R}$

# [Positive Sets](1770841079-MIOM.md)
> [!def] Positive Sets
> Let $P \neq \emptyset, P \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ be called the **set of positive real numbers**.
>
> **Properties of $P$:**
> 1.  If $a,b \in P$ then $a+b \in P$.
> 2.  If $a,b \in P$ then $a \cdot b \in P$.
> 3.  **Trichotomy Property:** If $a \in \mathbb{R}$, then exactly one of the following must be true:
>     * $a \in P$
>     * $a = 0$
>     * $-a \in P$
>
> **Notation:**
> * If $a \in P$, we say $a$ is positive ($a>0$).
> * If $-a \in P$, we say $a$ is negative ($a<0$).
> * If $a \in P \cup \{ 0 \}$, we say $a$ is non-negative ($a \geq 0$).
> * If $-a \in P \cup \{ 0 \}$, we say $a$ is non-positive ($a \leq 0$).
>
> > [!def] Ordering
> > * If $a-b \in P$, we write $a>b$ (or $b<a$).
> > * If $a-b \in P \cup \{ 0 \}$, we write $a \geq b$ (or $b \leq a$).
> >
> > For any two real numbers $a,b$ exactly one of the following holds:
> > * $a>b$
> > * $a<b$
> > * $a=b$
