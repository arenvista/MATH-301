# Sequential Criterion for Limits

Let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}$ and let $c$ be a cluster point of $A$. The following are equivalent:

1. $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$
2. For every sequence $(x_n)$ in $A$ with $x_n \neq c$ and $x_n \to c$, we have $f(x_n) \to L$

----
### **The Sequential Criterion for Limits**

**Theorem Statement**
Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}$, let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}$ be a function, and let $c \in \mathbb{R}$ be a cluster point (or limit point) of $A$. Then the following two statements are logically equivalent:

1. **The Functional Limit:** $$\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$$
2. **The Sequential Limit:** For every sequence $(x_n)$ in $A$ such that $x_n \neq c$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$ and $\lim_{n \to \infty} x_n = c$, the sequence of function values $(f(x_n))$ converges to $L$:
   $$\lim_{n \to \infty} f(x_n) = L$$

---

### **Intuition and Importance**

This theorem is a fundamental bridge in real analysis. It connects the "continuous" world of functions (which rely on $\epsilon-\delta$ definitions) to the "discrete" world of sequences (which rely on $\epsilon-N$ definitions). 

* **Why it matters:** Proving a limit exists using $\epsilon-\delta$ can sometimes be algebraically messy. This criterion allows you to translate a function limit problem into a sequence limit problem, granting you access to all the powerful algebraic limit theorems established for sequences.
* **The "Path" Analogy:** Statement 2 essentially says that no matter what "path" of points $(x_n)$ you take to approach $c$, the outputs $f(x_n)$ must always travel toward the exact same destination, $L$. 

---

### **The Sequential Criterion for Divergence (Corollary)**

While the theorem is great for proving limits *exist*, its contrapositive is arguably its most famous application—used to prove that a limit **does not exist**. 

To show that $\lim_{x \to c} f(x)$ does not exist, you only need to do one of the following:
1. Find a single sequence $(x_n) \to c$ (with $x_n \neq c$) where $(f(x_n))$ does not converge.
2. Find **two distinct sequences**, $(x_n)$ and $(y_n)$, that both converge to $c$ (with terms $\neq c$), but their function values converge to different limits:
   $$\lim_{n \to \infty} f(x_n) = L_1 \quad \text{and} \quad \lim_{n \to \infty} f(y_n) = L_2 \quad \text{where} \quad L_1 \neq L_2$$

*(A classic example is proving $\lim_{x \to 0} \sin(1/x)$ does not exist by picking one sequence of roots where the function equals $0$, and another sequence of peaks where the function equals $1$.)*

---

### **Proof of Equivalence**

To prove that 1 and 2 are equivalent, we must prove both directions: $(1 \implies 2)$ and $(2 \implies 1)$.

#### **Part 1: Prove $(1 \implies 2)$**
*Assume the limit of the function is $L$. We want to show any valid sequence $(x_n)$ mapped through $f$ also converges to $L$.*

1. Assume $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$. By the $\epsilon-\delta$ definition of a limit, for any $\epsilon > 0$, there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that for all $x \in A$, if $0 < |x - c| < \delta$, then $|f(x) - L| < \epsilon$.
2. Now, let $(x_n)$ be any sequence in $A$ such that $x_n \neq c$ and $x_n \to c$.
3. By the definition of sequence convergence, for the $\delta$ found above, there exists an integer $N$ such that for all $n \ge N$, $|x_n - c| < \delta$.
4. Since we are given that $x_n \neq c$, we know $0 < |x_n - c|$. 
5. Therefore, for all $n \ge N$, we have $0 < |x_n - c| < \delta$. 
6. Because the condition for our function limit is satisfied, it follows that $|f(x_n) - L| < \epsilon$ for all $n \ge N$.
7. Thus, by definition, $\lim_{n \to \infty} f(x_n) = L$.

#### **Part 2: Prove $(2 \implies 1)$**
*We prove this by contraposition (or contradiction). We assume Statement 1 is false, and show that this forces Statement 2 to be false.*

1. Assume $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) \neq L$. 
2. Negating the $\epsilon-\delta$ definition means there exists some specific $\epsilon_0 > 0$ such that for *every* $\delta > 0$, there is at least one $x \in A$ where $0 < |x - c| < \delta$ but $|f(x) - L| \ge \epsilon_0$.
3. Because this is true for *any* $\delta$, we can construct a sequence by systematically shrinking $\delta$. Let $\delta = 1/n$ for each $n \in \mathbb{N}$.
4. For each $n$, choose an $x_n \in A$ such that $0 < |x_n - c| < 1/n$, but $|f(x_n) - L| \ge \epsilon_0$.
5. We have now constructed a sequence $(x_n)$. Because $0 < |x_n - c| < 1/n$, the Squeeze Theorem guarantees that $\lim_{n \to \infty} x_n = c$, and $x_n \neq c$.
6. However, because $|f(x_n) - L| \ge \epsilon_0$ for all $n$, the sequence $(f(x_n))$ **cannot** converge to $L$. 
7. This directly contradicts Statement 2. Therefore, if Statement 2 is true, Statement 1 must also be true. $\blacksquare$

---

## Proof

### (1) $\implies$ (2):
Assume $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$. Let $(x_n)$ be a sequence in $A$ with $x_n \neq c$ and $x_n \to c$. We want to show $f(x_n) \to L$.

By the definition of the limit, for every $\epsilon > 0$, there exists $\delta = \delta(\epsilon) > 0$ such that for all $x \in A$ with $0 < |x-c| < \delta$, we have $|f(x) - L| < \epsilon$.

Since $x_n \to c$ and $x_n \neq c$, there exists $N$ such that for all $n \geq N$, $0 < |x_n - c| < \delta$. Thus, for $n \geq N$, $|f(x_n) - L| < \epsilon$. Therefore, $f(x_n) \to L$.

### (2) $\implies$ (1):
Assume for every sequence $x_n$ in $A$ with $x_n \neq c$ and $x_n \to c$, we have $f(x_n) \to L$. Suppose, for contradiction, that $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) \neq L$. Then, there exists $\epsilon_0 > 0$ such that for all $\delta > 0$, there exists $x \in A$ with $0 < |x - c| < \delta$ and $|f(x) - L| \geq \epsilon_0$.

For each $n \in \mathbb{N}$, take $\delta = 1/n$. Then there exists $x_n \in A$ with $0 < |x_n - c| < 1/n$ and $|f(x_n) - L| \geq \epsilon_0$. Then $x_n \to c$, $x_n \neq c$ for all $n$, but $|f(x_n) - L| \geq \epsilon_0$ for all $n$, so $f(x_n)$ does not converge to $L$. This contradicts our assumption. Thus, $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$.

---

**Conclusion:** $(1) \iff (2)$.

    \lim_{x \to 0} sin(\frac{1}{x}) ~\text{DNE} \in \mathbb{R}

Choose: $x_n = \frac{1}{2n \pi}$

$x_n \rightarrow 0$ as $n \rightarrow \infty$ and $x_n \neq 0, \forall~ n \in \mathbb{N}$

Thus $sin(\frac{1}{x_n}) = sin(2n \pi) = 0$

$y_n \rightarrow  0$ as $n \rightarrow  \infty$ and $y_n \neq 0, \forall~ n \in \mathbb{N}$ 

So: $(\frac{1}{y_n}) = sin(2n \pi + \frac{pi}{2}) = sin (\frac{pi}{2}) = 1$

## Applications in Limit Theorems


$$
\begin{gathered}
\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L 
\quad \quad
\lim_{x \to c} g(x) = M \\
\lim_{x \to c} \left( f(x) + g(x) \right) = L + M \\
\lim_{x \to c} f(g(x)) = LM \\
\lim_{x \to c} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{L}{M} \\
\lim_{x \to c} c \cdot f(x) = cL
\end{gathered}
$$ 


Proof: $\lim_{x \to c} \left( f(x) + g(x) \right) = L + M \\$

For every sequence $(x_n) \in A$ with $x_n \rightarrow c$ and $x_n \not\in c$, $\forall~  n \in \mathbb{N}$. 

BWS:
$$
\begin{gathered}
\lim_{x \to c} \left( f(x) + g(x) \right) = L + M  \\
\leftarrow \begin{cases}
f(x_n) \rightarrow L \\
g(x_n) \rightarrow M
\end{cases}
\end{gathered}
$$

Since $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$ by $(1) \implies (2)$, $f(x_n) \rightarrow L$

Since $\lim_{x \to c} g(x) = L$ by $(1) \implies (2)$, $g(x_n) \rightarrow M$

By limit theorem of sequences: $f(x_n) + g(x_n) \rightarrow L+M$

By $(2) \implies (1)$ we can say $\lim_{x \to c} f(x_n) + g(x_n) \rightarrow L+M$

## Squeeze Theorem for Functions

$$
\begin{gathered}
\forall~ x \in A ~\land~ x \neq c \quad \quad f(x) \leq g(x) \leq h(x) \\
\\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L ~\land~ \lim_{x \to c} h(x) = L \implies \lim_{x \to c} g(x)  \\
\end{gathered}
$$ 

### Proof:
$$
\begin{gathered}
\lim_{x \to c} f(x) =  L \overset{(1) \implies (2)}{\implies} f(x_n) \rightarrow L \\
\lim_{x \to c} h(x) =  L \overset{(1) \implies (2)}{\implies} h(x_n) \rightarrow L \\
\text{Observe } \forall~ n \in \mathbb{N}, ~ f(x_n) \leq  g(x_n) \leq h(x_n)
\end{gathered}
$$ 
By the sequeeze theorem on sequences: 
$$ \lim_{x \to c} g(x_n) = L \implies \lim_{x \to c} g(x) = L $$
