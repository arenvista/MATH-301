We say that $f$ is continous at $c \in A$ if $\forall~ \epsilon>0, \exists~ \delta_\epsilon > 0 ~s.t.~ \forall~$ 
$$
x \in A \land |x-c| < \delta_\epsilon \implies  |f(x)-f(c)|<\epsilon
$$ 

Differences:
- $c$ may be in $A$
- $x$ may equal $c$


If $c$ is a cluster point of $A$ then
$$
    \lim_{x \to c} f(x) = f(c)
$$ 

"The limit exists and the limit is $f(c)$"

If $c$ is not a cluster point of $A$

Then 
$$
    \exists~ \delta > 0, \forall~ x \in  A, ~ \land x \neq c , |x-c| \geq \delta
$$ 

In other words $c$ is "isolated"

If C is isolated, then $f$ is automatically continous at $c$.


Ex: if $f: \mathbb{N} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is continous, then $f$ is continous on $\mathbb{N}$.

## Theorem: Sequential Criterion for Continunity 

A function $f: A \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is continous at a point $c \in A \iff \forall~ (x_n) \in A$ that converges to $c$, the sequence $(f(x_n))$ converges to $f(c)$



Corollary:
$f$ is not continous at $c \in  A \iff \exists~ (x_n)$ with $x_n \rightarrow c \land  f(x_n) \not \rightarrow f(c)$

x \text{ is rational } \implies  1\\

    \end{cases}
$$
