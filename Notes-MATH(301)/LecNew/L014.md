# For Quiz 

$$
\overline{\lim}_{n\to\infty} x_n \;=\; \lim_{n\to\infty} s_n,
\qquad
s_n \;:=\; \sup_{m\ge n} x_m
$$

$$
\overline{\lim}_{n\to\infty} (x_n+y_n)
\;\le\;
\overline{\lim}_{n\to\infty} x_n
\;+\;
\overline{\lim}_{n\to\infty} y_n
$$

Equivalently:

$$
\limsup_{n\to\infty} (x_n+y_n)
\;\le\;
\limsup_{n\to\infty} x_n
\;+\;
\limsup_{n\to\infty} y_n
$$

Key inequality (for each fixed $n$):

$$
\sup_{m\ge n}(x_m+y_m)
\;\le\;
\sup_{m\ge n} x_m
\;+\;
\sup_{m\ge n} y_m
$$

Note (for all $m\ge n$):

$$
x_m \le \sup_{k\ge n} x_k
\qquad \forall\, m\ge n
$$

$$
y_m \le \sup_{k\ge n} y_k
\qquad \forall\, m\ge n
$$

Recall:
$$
\begin{align}
\underline{\lim} x_n & = -\overline{\lim}(-x_n) \\
& = -\lim_{n \to \infty} \sup_{n \leq m} (-x_m) \\
& = - \lim_{n \to \infty} -\inf_{n \leq m} \\
& = \lim_{n \to \infty} \inf x_m
\end{align}
$$

Show the following Converges:
$$
x_n = \frac{1}{1^2} + \frac{1}{2^2} + \dots + \frac{1}{n^2}
$$ 

Recall (Class Example):
$$
\frac{1}{1^p} + \frac{1}{2^p} + \dots + \frac{1}{n^p}
$$ 

$$
\frac{1}{2^p} \leq p
$$ 

$$
\begin{gathered}
\frac{1}{2^p} \leq \int_{1}^{2} \frac{1}{x^p} \, dx  \\
\frac{1}{3^p} \leq \int_{2}^{3} \frac{1}{x^p} \, dx  \\
\end{gathered}
$$ 

So we can say:
$$
x_n^p \leq 
1 + 
\int_{1}^{2} \frac{1}{x^p} \, dx +
\int_{2}^{3} \frac{1}{x^p} \, dx +
\dots  +
\int_{n}^{n-1} \frac{1}{x^p} \, dx 
$$ 

$$
    = 1 + \int_{1}^{n} \frac{1}{x^p} \, dx \leq  
1 + \int_{1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{x^p} \, dx = 1 + 0 - \frac{1}{1-p} 
$$ 
$$
    = 1 + \frac{1}{p-1} = \frac{p}{p-1}
$$

Cauchy Criterion / Monotone Convergence:

Show that the sequence defined by
$$
x_{n+1}=\sqrt{2+x_n}
$$
converges.

### 1) Monotone increasing
Prove by induction that
$$
x_n \le x_{n+1}\quad \text{for all } n.
$$
Assuming $x_k \le x_{k+1}$, then
$$
2+x_k \le 2+x_{k+1}.
$$
Since $\sqrt{\cdot}$ is increasing on $[0,\infty)$,
$$
\sqrt{2+x_k}\le \sqrt{2+x_{k+1}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x_{k+1}\le x_{k+2}.
$$
Thus $(x_n)$ is monotone increasing.

### 2) Bounded above
Also show by induction that
$$
x_n \le 2 \quad \text{for all } n,
$$
so the sequence is increasing and bounded, hence convergent.

---

### 3) Alternative: Cauchy (contraction) argument
Show the iteration is contractive:
$$
|x_{n+2}-x_{n+1}|
= \left|\sqrt{2+x_{n+1}}-\sqrt{2+x_n}\right|.
$$
Rationalizing,
$$
|x_{n+2}-x_{n+1}|
= \frac{|x_{n+1}-x_n|}{\sqrt{2+x_{n+1}}+\sqrt{2+x_n}}.
$$
If $x_n\ge 0$ (e.g., for nonnegative initial data), then
$$
\sqrt{2+x_{n+1}}+\sqrt{2+x_n}\ge 2\sqrt{2},
$$
so
$$
|x_{n+2}-x_{n+1}|
\le \frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\,|x_{n+1}-x_n|.
$$
Hence $(x_n)$ is Cauchy and therefore convergent.

If the sequence is defined by
$$
x_1=a>0,\qquad x_{n+1}=x_n+\frac{1}{x_n},
$$
then
$$
x_{n+1}-x_n=\frac{1}{x_n}\ge 0,
$$
so $\{x_n\}$ is monotone increasing.

Compute the first few terms:
$$
\begin{aligned}
x_1&=a,\\
x_2&=a+\frac{1}{a},\\
x_3&=a+\frac{1}{a}+\frac{1}{a+\frac{1}{a}}
    =a+\frac{1}{a}+\frac{a}{a^2+1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

If $x_n\to L$ for some finite $L$, then taking limits in the recurrence gives
$$
L=\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n+1}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(x_n+\frac{1}{x_n}\right)=L+\frac{1}{L},
$$
which is impossible for finite $L$. Hence the sequence has no finite limit and, since it is increasing,
$$
x_n\to +\infty.
$$
